§ B-I.1

Double Diamond Model Extra

FrameworkK28 min

The Double Diamond is the British Design Council's 2005 model of the design process. It offers a broader view than the 5-stage model — showing how design moves through two expand–contract cycles.

📘 Definition

A framework with four phases — Discover · Define · Develop · Deliver — grouped into two diamonds. Each diamond starts wide (divergent thinking) and ends narrow (convergent thinking).

   Discover          Define    │    Develop          Deliver
   ╲              ╱            │    ╲              ╱
    ╲   DIAMOND  ╱             │     ╲   DIAMOND  ╱
     ╲    1    ╱              │      ╲    2    ╱
      ╲       ╱               │       ╲       ╱
          ▼                   │           ▼
      Problem Space           │       Solution Space

The four phases

  1. Discover — explore widely. Observe, interview, gather insights. Diverge.
  2. Define — narrow to the real problem. Write a clear brief. Converge.
  3. Develop — explore many solutions. Ideate, sketch, prototype. Diverge.
  4. Deliver — narrow to the best one. Test, refine, launch. Converge.

How it differs from the 5-stage model

5-Stage ModelDouble Diamond
Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → TestDiscover → Define → Develop → Deliver
Tight, linear-feeling stagesTwo clear "expand and contract" cycles
Focus on user and solutionSeparates problem space from solution space
🇬🇧 UK Design Council · Original use
The Double Diamond was created after the Council studied 11 companies and found they all moved through two cycles: first to understand the problem, then to shape the solution. Companies that skipped the first diamond — and jumped straight to ideas — consistently failed.
Get the problem right before you build the solution.
✅ Tip

Use the Double Diamond when you have a long project. Use the 5-stage model for a workshop or a class sprint.

⚠️ Watch out

The two diamonds are not a strict sequence. Real projects loop back — you may re-enter Discover after a failed Develop phase.

§ B-I.2

Design Sprint Extra

MethodK27 min

The Design Sprint is a 5-day process invented at Google Ventures by Jake Knapp. It compresses months of design work into one week — ending with a working prototype tested on real users by Friday.

📘 Definition

A structured five-day workshop that takes a team from a problem to a tested prototype in one week. Each day has a specific, tightly scoped deliverable.

The five days

DayNameWhat happens
MondayMapUnderstand the problem. Interview experts. Choose a focus.
TuesdaySketchEach person sketches solutions silently. No groupthink.
WednesdayDecideTeam critiques and picks the best ideas. Storyboard the prototype.
ThursdayPrototypeBuild a realistic facade — mock-up, video, or role-play.
FridayTest5 real users use the prototype. Team watches. Insights by evening.

Why it works

  • Time-boxing forces decisions instead of endless debate.
  • Silent sketching removes the loudest-voice problem.
  • Prototype over discussion — you learn more from a Friday test than a month of meetings.
  • One team, one room — no context switching.
☕ Blue Bottle Coffee · GV Design Sprint
Blue Bottle Coffee used a Design Sprint to redesign their online store. Instead of a 6-month roadmap, they had a tested prototype in 5 days. Conversion improved, and they rolled out the design within weeks.
Compression beats perfection when the problem is well-scoped.
💡 Example

A campus team wants to redesign the canteen app. Monday: interview 5 students. Tuesday: each member sketches 3 ideas. Wednesday: pick the best. Thursday: build a Figma mock-up. Friday: test with 5 users, log feedback, decide next steps.

⚠️ Watch out

Design Sprints work for focused problems. They fail if the problem is too vague or needs months of research. Do your Discover work first.

§ B-I.3

Cultural & Contextual Design Extra

ConceptK38 min

Most Design Thinking literature comes from Western contexts. But users in India, Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America live differently — joint families, shared devices, multiple languages, price sensitivity, and strong community bonds.

📘 Definition

Designing for a specific cultural and social context — considering family structure, language, festivals, device sharing, price sensitivity, and local rituals — not just translating a Western design.

What changes in the Indian context

  • Shared devices — one phone used by 4–6 family members. Logins, personalisation, notifications all become tricky.
  • Language diversity — 22 official languages. English-only design excludes a huge population.
  • Trust networks — recommendations from family and community outweigh ads.
  • Price sensitivity — a ₹50 difference changes behaviour. Free tiers dominate.
  • Festivals and seasons — purchase behaviour swings sharply around Diwali, harvests, and school terms.
  • Informal economy — a huge share of users buy in cash, from local shops, not online.
💬 WhatsApp · Family Groups in India
WhatsApp became India's default communication app not because of any "Indian feature" but because the group feature mapped naturally to joint-family communication. Later India-first features (WhatsApp Pay, business catalogues) doubled down on that fit.
Fit the local ritual, and adoption follows.
💰 Paytm · Regional Language UI
Paytm invested heavily in supporting 10+ Indian languages early. While competitors had English-only interfaces, Paytm's regional UIs let first-time internet users complete payments without a translator. That choice built rural adoption.
Language is not a translation — it's access.
✅ How to practise it

Before designing for Indian users, walk through their day: who do they live with, what language do they think in, what device do they share, what do they trust?

⚠️ Watch out

Don't assume "Indian users" are one group. A college student in Bengaluru and a farmer in Bihar may share a country but nothing else.

§ B-I.4

Abstraction Laddering Extra

MethodK36 min

Abstraction Laddering is a technique for moving between concrete and abstract views of a problem. It helps teams decide what level to design at.

📘 Definition

A two-way laddering technique: ask "Why?" to move up the ladder toward abstract goals, and "How?" to move down toward concrete implementations.

The ladder — a live example

Level 5 (abstract)  ▸ Self-care                ▲ Why?
Level 4             ▸ Feeling comfortable
Level 3             ▸ Warm feet
Level 2             ▸ Wear socks
Level 1 (concrete)  ▸ Buy woollen socks         ▼ How?

How to use it

  1. Start with the user's stated problem ("I need new socks").
  2. Ask "Why?" 3–5 times to move up. What's the deeper goal?
  3. Ask "How?" 3–5 times to move down. What's the concrete action?
  4. Decide which level is the right one for your project.
💡 Example — Café redesign

Customer says: "I need faster Wi-Fi."

  • Up: why? "So I can work from here." → why? "Because home is distracting." → why? "I want quiet focus."
  • Down: how? "Faster router." → how? "Better seating near the router." → how? "A dedicated quiet corner."

The real problem wasn't Wi-Fi — it was a quiet workspace.

✅ Tip

If you solve the "up" problem (quiet focus), you may not even need the "down" solution (faster router).

⚠️ Watch out

Going too far up makes the problem impossible to solve ("human happiness"). Stop when the level feels actionable.

§ B-I.5

Design Brief Extra

ToolK37 min

A Design Brief is a one-page document that anchors a project from start to finish. Without it, teams drift, clients change their mind, and the project never converges.

📘 Definition

A short, written agreement between a designer and a client (or a team and itself) that states the problem, target user, constraints, deliverables, timeline, and success criteria.

Anatomy of a design brief

SectionWhat it answers
ProblemWhat are we solving? (in one sentence)
UserWho is it for? (the persona)
ContextWhere and when will it be used?
ConstraintsBudget, time, tech, legal limits
DeliverablesWhat will we hand over?
Success criteriaHow will we know it worked?
TimelineKey dates and milestones
💡 Example brief — Campus Canteen App
  • Problem: Second-year students lose 10+ minutes daily to canteen queues.
  • User: 18–22-year-old students with 20-minute lunch breaks.
  • Context: Campus canteen, 12:30–1:00 pm peak.
  • Constraints: ₹0 budget; basic Android phones; 3 weeks.
  • Deliverables: Clickable Figma prototype + user test report.
  • Success: Task completion under 60 sec; 4/5 users prefer it.
  • Timeline: W1 research · W2 build · W3 test + present.
✅ Tip

Write the brief after the Define stage. It should reflect what you learned — not what the client assumed on day one.

⚠️ Watch out

A brief is a promise, not a contract. If user research reveals a different problem, reframe and re-approve.

§ B-I.6

Reframing Extra

MindsetK37 min

Reframing means redefining the problem — often because the original framing was wrong.

📘 Definition

The act of re-examining and changing how a problem is stated. The change feels small, but it opens a completely different solution space.

The classic example

💡 The slow elevator

Tenants complain the elevators are too slow.

  • Original frame: "The elevators are too slow." → New motors. Cost: ₹50 lakh.
  • Reframed: "The wait feels too long." → Install mirrors in the lobby. Cost: ₹500. Complaints stopped.

The problem was never the elevator's speed — it was the experience of waiting.

How to reframe

  1. Notice frustration. If a problem feels stuck, the frame is probably wrong.
  2. Ask "Why is this a problem?" Dig for the underlying need.
  3. Invert it. "How could we make the problem worse?"
  4. Change the actor. If the frame blames the user, flip to blaming the design.
  5. Change the scale. Zoom in or zoom out.
🎧 Spotify · "Playlists, not albums"
Music had always been framed as "albums". Spotify reframed the problem as "delivering the right song for the moment" — which led to Discover Weekly, algorithmic playlists, and a fundamentally different product.
Change the frame, change the product.
✅ Tip

Reframe after empathy work and before ideation. It's the moment when insight turns into a problem statement.

⚠️ Watch out

Reframing is not "changing the topic". If the new frame doesn't serve the same users or need, it's avoidance, not reframing.

§ B-I.7

Design Ethics Extra

ConceptK49 min

Unit I covers research ethics (consent, privacy). But design ethics is broader — the responsibility of a designer for the long-term impact of what they ship.

📘 Definition

The moral principles that guide design decisions — beyond legal compliance — covering user agency, honest interfaces, accessibility, sustainability, and societal impact.

1. Dark patterns

Interfaces that trick users into things they don't want.

  • Roach motel — easy to subscribe, impossible to cancel.
  • Confirmshaming — "No thanks, I like paying full price."
  • Hidden costs — fees revealed only at checkout.
  • Misdirection — Cancel tiny and grey; Upgrade big and colourful.

2. Accessibility

Designing for users with disabilities — vision, hearing, motor, cognitive. Ignoring it excludes ~15% of the world.

  • Contrast ratios that work for low vision.
  • Keyboard-navigable interfaces.
  • Alt text for images.
  • Captions and transcripts for video.

3. User agency

Do users control their experience, or does the product? Infinite scroll, autoplay, and manipulative notifications all weaken agency.

4. Sustainability

Digital products have carbon footprints. Heavy sites, autoplay videos, and unnecessary computation cost the planet.

5. Societal impact

What happens at scale? A feature that works for one user may harm millions when used 500 million times a day.

📱 Facebook · Infinite Scroll
Infinite scroll increased engagement metrics dramatically — but former Facebook executives have publicly said it contributed to compulsive use and mental-health harm. The company's own research flagged the issue. The design worked, but it was ethically contested.
A design that succeeds at its metric can still fail ethically.
✅ How to practise it

Before shipping, run the "regret test": would a user, an hour later, regret the choice this design nudged them into? If yes, redesign.

⚠️ Watch out

Ethics is not "just be nice". Some features that feel kind in the short term — constant notifications, easy sharing — cause harm in the long term.
