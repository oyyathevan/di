§ B-II.1

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) Extra

FrameworkK38 min

JTBD is a way of understanding users not by who they are, but by what they're trying to accomplish. People don't "want" a drill — they want a hole in the wall.

📘 Definition

A framework that reframes every purchase as "hiring" a product to do a job. It focuses on the progress a user wants to make in a specific circumstance.

The core insight

Customers don't buy products — they hire them to make progress. When a better "employee" comes along for the same job, they switch.

💡 Classic example — Milkshake

McDonald's studied milkshake sales. Interviews didn't help. But observing when people bought revealed the pattern: half the shakes were bought before 9 am by solo drivers with long commutes. They were hiring the milkshake to make a boring drive interesting and stay full until lunch.

Competing products weren't other milkshakes — they were bananas, bagels, and coffee. That reframing changed the entire product strategy.

The JTBD statement format

When [situation],
I want to [motivation],
So I can [expected outcome].
💡 Campus example

When I'm between two back-to-back classes, I want to grab a quick snack without waiting in line, so I can reach the next lecture on time.

JTBD vs Persona

PersonaJTBD
Answers "who is the user?"Answers "what job are they hiring us for?"
Demographics, habits, goalsCircumstance, motivation, progress
One persona = one type of personOne job = many kinds of people
Best for design directionBest for product strategy
🚗 Uber · "Get from A to B"
Uber's JTBD wasn't "ride-hailing". It was "get from A to B safely, quickly, at a fair price". That job competes with taxis, buses, personal cars, bikes — not just other apps.
Define your job broadly, or you'll miss your real competition.
✅ Tip

Interview users during the moment of purchase. Memory re-writes motivation. What they say they did last month is less reliable than what they're doing right now.

⚠️ Watch out

JTBD is not a persona replacement — it's a complement. Use personas to design the experience; use JTBD to decide which product to build.

§ B-II.2

Kano Model Extra

ToolK37 min

The Kano Model classifies product features by how much they affect customer satisfaction. It replaces the assumption "more features = happier customers".

📘 Definition

A model that sorts features into five categories — Must-be, Performance, Attractive, Indifferent, Reverse — each affecting satisfaction differently.

The five categories

CategoryMeaningIf missingIf present
Must-beBasic expectationsFuriousNeutral (taken for granted)
PerformanceMore is betterUnhappySatisfied — scales linearly
AttractiveDelightersNeutralWow!
IndifferentNot noticedNeutralNeutral
ReverseSome want it, some don'tRelief for someAnger for others
💡 Example — Food delivery app
  • Must-be: Food arrives; app works.
  • Performance: Delivery speed; number of restaurants.
  • Attractive: Live GPS tracking (in 2018); AI meal suggestions.
  • Indifferent: Dark mode toggle.
  • Reverse: Frequent push notifications — some love them, some hate them.

Why it matters

  • Stop over-investing in Must-be features — no one thanks you for them.
  • Invest in Attractive features that create delight — they drive word of mouth.
  • Watch for features that drift from Attractive to Performance to Must-be over time (e.g., GPS tracking on delivery apps).
📱 Smartphone camera
A camera was once an Attractive feature (delight). Then it became Performance (megapixels sold phones). Now it's Must-be (no camera = no sale). The same feature shifted categories over 20 years.
Feature categories are not permanent.
✅ Tip

Use Kano to decide where to invest engineering effort — not to decide what to build at all. Combine with JTBD for the full picture.

⚠️ Watch out

Kano is based on user surveys — and users are bad at predicting their own future feelings. Validate with behaviour, not just answers.

§ B-II.3

Fishbone (Ishikawa) Diagram Extra

ToolK37 min

The Fishbone Diagram (also called the Ishikawa Diagram or Cause-and-Effect Diagram) organises many possible causes of a problem into visual categories.

📘 Definition

A visual tool that lists every possible cause of a problem grouped under standard categories, arranged to look like a fish skeleton — problem at the head, causes on the bones.

Classic 6 categories (6 M's)

  • Man (people) — skills, training, motivation
  • Machine — equipment, tools, tech
  • Method — process, steps, procedure
  • Material — inputs, quality, source
  • Measurement — data, metrics, feedback
  • Mother Nature — environment, timing, weather

Visual

  Man        Machine       Method
   ╲           │             ╱
    ╲          │            ╱
     ╲─────────┼───────────╱────────▶ PROBLEM
     ╱         │            ╲
    ╱          │             ╲
   ╱           │              ╲
Material   Measurement    Environment
💡 Example — Campus canteen queue

Problem: queue takes too long at lunch.

  • Man: Only 2 servers at peak.
  • Machine: Old billing computer freezes.
  • Method: Orders taken one at a time, verbally.
  • Material: Cash-only — no UPI option.
  • Measurement: No visible wait-time info.
  • Environment: Peak hour 12:45–1:00 pm, single counter.

Fishbone vs 5 Whys

5 WhysFishbone
Single chain of causesMany parallel causes
Deep-dive one branchWide map of all branches
Fast — 10 minThorough — 30+ min
Best for one root causeBest for complex problems with many causes
🏭 Toyota Production System
Ishikawa developed the diagram at Kawasaki Steel in the 1960s. It became a standard quality-control tool in Japanese manufacturing, and Toyota embedded it into its world-famous production system.
Good tools travel — Fishbone crossed from steel to software to services.
✅ Tip

Use Fishbone before 5 Whys. The fishbone surfaces all candidates; the 5 Whys then drills into the most likely one.

⚠️ Watch out

Don't stop at listing causes. The value comes from picking 2–3 to investigate further with data.

§ B-II.4

Pareto Analysis (80/20 Rule) Extra

ToolK35 min

In most systems, a small number of causes produce most of the effect. Pareto Analysis finds that small number so you can focus effort where it matters.

📘 Definition

The observation that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes. The Pareto chart ranks causes by impact so the top few stand out.

Where the 80/20 rule shows up

  • 20% of customers generate 80% of revenue.
  • 20% of bugs cause 80% of crashes.
  • 20% of features are used 80% of the time.
  • 20% of complaints come from 80% of a specific problem type.
💡 Example — Campus complaints

A college administration logs 200 complaints in a semester. Sorting them reveals:

  • Canteen queues — 62 complaints
  • Wi-Fi reliability — 48 complaints
  • Library timings — 35 complaints
  • Classroom AC — 20 complaints
  • Everything else — 35 complaints

The top 3 categories = 73% of all complaints. Fix those three, and the college covers most of the frustration.

How to build a Pareto chart

  1. List all problem categories.
  2. Count the frequency of each.
  3. Sort descending.
  4. Compute cumulative percentage.
  5. Mark where the cumulative curve hits ~80%.
📞 Microsoft · Bugs
A famous Microsoft internal study found that fixing the top 20% of reported bugs eliminated 80% of user crashes. Teams stopped trying to fix every bug and started fixing the right ones.
Prioritisation is a design skill, not just a management one.
✅ Tip

Pareto is not a law — sometimes it's 90/10, sometimes 70/30. The point is that the distribution is almost never flat.

⚠️ Watch out

Don't blindly ignore the long tail. In safety-critical systems (aviation, medicine), rare events can matter more than frequent ones.

§ B-II.5

Systems Thinking Extra

MindsetK49 min

Systems Thinking sees problems as part of interconnected systems — not isolated events. It asks: what else is connected to this, and what happens when I change one part?

📘 Definition

An approach that views a problem as a system of parts — actors, flows, feedback loops — rather than a single cause-effect chain. It focuses on relationships, not just parts.

Key ideas

  • Feedback loops — a change in A affects B, which affects A back.
  • Delays — effects appear much later than causes.
  • Emergence — the system behaves in ways no single part predicts.
  • Leverage points — small interventions that shift the whole system.
💡 Example — Traffic jam

Adding more lanes often increases traffic. Why? Because wider roads encourage more driving, which fills the new capacity — a feedback loop. The real fix is usually in public transport, work-from-home policies, or staggered timings.

🚦 Waze · Induced demand
Waze's traffic routing creates a system effect: as users are diverted off highways, side streets fill with traffic, causing new jams on roads that weren't previously congested. The system adapts. Simple cause-effect reasoning missed this.
In a system, your fix becomes someone else's problem.

Systems Thinking vs Linear Thinking

LinearSystems
A causes BA ↔ B (mutual influence)
Fix the eventFix the structure that produces the event
Fast, simpleSlow, thorough
Works for isolated problemsWorks for wicked problems
✅ Tip

Ask "And then what?" three times. Every solution has second-order effects. Systems Thinking is about seeing those before you ship.

⚠️ Watch out

Don't use systems language as an excuse to avoid decisions. At some point, you still have to act.

§ B-II.6

Laddering (Why → How) Extra

MethodK36 min

Laddering is a structured questioning technique to move from surface wants to deeper goals — or from abstract goals down to concrete choices. It's a close cousin of Abstraction Laddering (Unit I extra).

📘 Definition

A research technique where you repeatedly ask "Why?" to uncover deeper motivations, or "How?" to uncover concrete preferences — moving up and down a ladder of meaning.

Up-laddering (values)

Move from product attributes → functional consequences → emotional values.

Attribute    ▸ Buys an iPhone
Consequence  ▸ Takes good photos
Goal         ▸ Wants to remember trips
Value        ▸ Feels close to family
             ▲ Why?  ▲ Why?  ▲ Why?

Down-laddering (choices)

Move from abstract values down to specific product requirements.

Value        ▸ Wants to feel safe online
Goal         ▸ Protect personal data
Consequence  ▸ Use strong passwords
Attribute    ▸ Wants a password manager
             ▼ How?  ▼ How?  ▼ How?
💡 Interview question chain
  • Q: Why do you use this app? → "Because it's easy."
  • Why does easy matter? → "Because I use it when I'm rushing."
  • Why are you rushing? → "Because I'm always late for class."
  • Why does lateness bother you? → "Because I don't want to seem unreliable."

The real value isn't "easy" — it's reputation. That changes the design brief entirely.

🍫 Cadbury · Values laddering
When Cadbury asked why people bought chocolate, answers ranged from "taste" to "gift". Laddering revealed the deepest value: belonging — sharing chocolate is an act of connection. That insight shaped the advertising for decades.
Ladder up to value, then design back down.
✅ Tip

Stop laddering when the answer becomes "because that's just who I am" or "I don't know" — that's the edge of useful insight.

⚠️ Watch out

Rapid-fire "why" can feel like an interrogation. Ask gently, and share the reasoning with the user.

§ B-II.7

Reframing the Problem Extra

MindsetK47 min

Reframing the problem is the deepest skill in Design Thinking. The way you frame a problem determines the solutions you can imagine. Change the frame, and a whole new solution space opens.

📘 Definition

Deliberately restating a problem in a different way — changing the user, the goal, the scale, or the assumption — to unlock new approaches.

Four ways to reframe

  1. Change the user. "How do we make students read more?" → "How do we make reading less effort than scrolling?"
  2. Change the goal. "How do we reduce queues?" → "How do we make waiting feel shorter?"
  3. Change the scale. "How do we fix India's traffic?" → "How do we make one Bengaluru junction flow better?"
  4. Change the assumption. "How do we sell more books?" → "How do we sell reading experiences?"
📖 Amazon Kindle · Reframed reading
Books were a product (physical object). Amazon reframed the problem: "How do we let people carry every book they'll ever read?" Kindle wasn't a better book — it was a new framing of what reading could be.
The best innovations reframe, not improve.
🚗 Ford · Reframed "faster horses"
Henry Ford didn't build a faster horse. He reframed: "How do we move people faster than animals can?" The frame flip made the automobile obvious.
Customers describe solutions; designers must find the real problem behind them.

How to reframe systematically

  1. Listen for the emotional complaint behind the functional one.
  2. Ask: "If this problem were solved, what would be different?"
  3. Ask: "What if the constraint disappeared?"
  4. Ask: "What is the opposite of this problem?"
  5. Rewrite the problem statement from three different angles.
  6. Pick the frame that opens the most interesting solutions.
✅ Tip

Reframe before ideation, never after. If you reframe during ideation, the team loses momentum. Make it a defined step between Define and Ideate.

⚠️ Watch out

Reframing is powerful but risky. If the new frame doesn't serve the same user with the same underlying need, you've changed the problem, not reframed it.
