§ B-III.1

Six Thinking Hats Extra

MethodK28 min

Six Thinking Hats is Edward de Bono's technique for structuring a group discussion. Instead of everyone thinking in different directions at once, the group wears one "hat" at a time.

📘 Definition

A parallel-thinking method where the whole group consciously shifts between six mental modes — one at a time — to explore a problem from every angle without argument.

The six hats

HatModeFocuses on
⚪ WhiteFactsData, information, what we know
🔴 RedFeelingsEmotions, gut reactions, intuition
⚫ BlackCautionRisks, weaknesses, what could go wrong
🟡 YellowOptimismBenefits, value, what could go right
🟢 GreenCreativityNew ideas, alternatives, possibilities
🔵 BlueProcessManaging the thinking, summarising

How a session flows

  1. Blue hat opens — "We're deciding between 3 packaging designs."
  2. White hat — what data do we have? Costs, recyclability, print time.
  3. Green hat — new ideas; combine, adapt, modify.
  4. Yellow hat — benefits of each option.
  5. Black hat — risks, downsides.
  6. Red hat — gut feelings — no justification allowed.
  7. Blue hat closes — decision and next steps.
💡 Why it works

Usually, one person is always critical, one always optimistic, one always emotional. Six Hats forces everyone into the same mode — no one gets stuck in their role.

🏢 Siemens · Corporate adoption
Siemens famously reduced a 30-day meeting to 2 days after adopting Six Thinking Hats. The tool cut meeting time by focusing the group on one mode at a time.
Structure beats speed — but they often end up together.
✅ Tip

Use Green hat after White and before Black. Generating ideas before critiquing them keeps creativity alive.

⚠️ Watch out

Six Hats works best in groups of 5–12. In smaller groups it can feel forced; in larger groups the hat-discipline breaks down.

§ B-III.2

Crazy 8s Extra

MethodK26 min

Crazy 8s is a fast sketching exercise. You fold a paper into eight sections, then draw eight different ideas in eight minutes — one per minute.

📘 Definition

A rapid ideation technique: 8 sketches in 8 minutes, one idea per panel. Forces quantity over quality and bypasses perfectionism.

How to run it

  1. Fold an A4 sheet into 8 panels (or use a printed template).
  2. Set a timer for 8 minutes.
  3. Sketch one rough idea per panel — a minute per sketch.
  4. No erasing, no refining, no text — just shapes and arrows.
  5. At the end, share sketches with the team.
  6. Dot-vote the strongest 2–3.
💡 Example — Redesigning a checkout flow

Eight sketches in eight minutes might include: one-tap purchase, QR scan, voice order, pre-loaded wallet, social gifting, subscription, WhatsApp order, in-store pickup. Only by generating this many can the team find the one that actually fits.

🎨 GV Design Sprint
Crazy 8s is a standard step in Google Ventures' Design Sprint. On Tuesday morning, each team member creates their own Crazy 8s before group discussion. It flattens hierarchy — the best ideas often come from the quietest person.
Speed creates freedom; slowness creates polish that usually gets rejected anyway.
✅ Tip

Sketch with a thick marker, not a pencil. Thick marker lines prevent you from adding detail — which is exactly the point.

⚠️ Watch out

Crazy 8s works for product features, interface flows, and physical objects. It works poorly for abstract problems like "how do we change company culture".

§ B-III.3

How-Wow-Now Matrix Extra

ToolK36 min

The How-Wow-Now Matrix sorts ideas into three buckets by how realistic and impactful they are.

📘 Definition

A triage tool for ideas: Now = easy and immediate; Wow = exciting but harder; How = hard and unclear — needs research.

The three buckets

BucketMeaningAction
NowDoable today with existing resourcesStart now
WowHigh-impact but needs effortPlan a sprint
HowGreat idea, unclear pathResearch first
💡 Example — Campus Wi-Fi problems
  • Now: Publish a live status page for outages.
  • Wow: Install mesh routers across hostels.
  • How: Partner with a telco for free student data — needs negotiation and a real feasibility study.
🏫 Google Design Sprints
Teams use How-Wow-Now at the end of ideation to hand off ideas to the right owner: Now ideas go to engineering, Wow ideas become sprint briefs, How ideas go to research.
Every idea has a home — that's what makes workshops productive.
✅ Tip

Balance the three buckets. If 90% of ideas are "Now", the team isn't dreaming big enough. If 90% are "How", they're not shipping anything.

⚠️ Watch out

"Wow" and "How" are not rejections. They're deferrals. Log them so they don't get lost.

§ B-III.4

Idea Napkin Pitch Extra

ToolK37 min

The Idea Napkin is a one-page format for capturing an idea so clearly that it can be understood in 30 seconds — as if sketched on the back of a napkin.

📘 Definition

A one-page concept summary with six sections: Who, Why, What, How, Value, and Next Step. Designed to be both memorable and shareable.

The six sections

SectionQuestion
WhoWho is this for?
WhyWhat problem does it solve?
WhatWhat is the idea, in one sentence?
HowHow does it work at a high level?
ValueWhy is it better than the current option?
NextWhat's the very next thing to test?
💡 Example — SkipQ campus app
  • Who: Second-year students with 20-minute lunch breaks.
  • Why: They lose 10+ minutes to canteen queues daily.
  • What: A pre-order app with a QR pickup counter.
  • How: Students scan a menu QR, pre-pay, and collect at a designated slot.
  • Value: Reduces queue time by 80%; costs the canteen nothing extra.
  • Next: Test with 5 students + 1 canteen partner this week.
💼 Sequoia Capital · Idea Napkin
Sequoia's famous "napkin" (a one-page business model sketch) is a direct ancestor of the Idea Napkin. Many early-stage startups used it to pitch investors in under a minute.
If you can't say it on one page, you don't understand it yet.
✅ Tip

Write the Napkin after an ideation round but before prototyping. It forces clarity at the exact moment clarity matters most.

⚠️ Watch out

The Napkin is a summary, not a business plan. Don't try to fit financials, GTM strategy, and team bios onto it.

§ B-III.5

Blue Sky Thinking Extra

MindsetK26 min

Blue Sky Thinking deliberately ignores all constraints — budget, time, technology, physics — and asks: "If anything were possible, what would the ideal solution look like?"

📘 Definition

A no-constraints ideation technique used to explore ideal futures before pulling back to feasibility. Removes the "yes, but…" reflex from the room.

How to run it

  1. State the problem clearly.
  2. Say out loud: "For the next 20 minutes, money, time, and technology do not exist."
  3. Generate the ideal solution — imagined.
  4. List what would have to be true to make it possible.
  5. Pull back to reality: which pieces are achievable now?
💡 Example — Classroom attendance

Blue sky: Every student is auto-marked by facial recognition the moment they enter; attendance data streams live to the professor.

What would need to be true: Reliable camera, privacy approval, low-cost hardware, GDPR/DPDP compliance.

Pull back: Start with QR check-in per seat — same outcome, achievable now.

🚀 SpaceX · Reusable rockets
In 2005, "reusable rockets" was blue-sky thinking. Every expert said it was impossible. SpaceX's blue-sky ideal became a roadmap — and today, landing a rocket is routine.
Blue sky isn't fantasy — it's the roadmap before the roadmap.
✅ Tip

Blue sky is best after convergent thinking, not before. If you blue-sky before you know the constraints, the ideas are useless.

⚠️ Watch out

Don't skip the "pull back" step. Blue sky without a return path becomes a daydream, not a design approach.

§ B-III.6

Random Entry / Random Word Extra

MethodK25 min

Random Entry is a creativity technique from Edward de Bono. Pick a random word, object, or image — then force a connection to your problem.

📘 Definition

A lateral-thinking method where you introduce an unrelated stimulus (usually a random noun) and connect it to the problem to generate unexpected ideas.

How it works

  1. State the problem: "How do we make students attend morning lectures?"
  2. Pick a random word — say, "cactus".
  3. List attributes of a cactus — spiky, survives with little water, slow-growing, flowers rarely.
  4. Force connections: What if lectures were "rare flowers" — surprise events students wouldn't want to miss?
  5. Explore: guest lectures, secret topics revealed only at the door, flash quizzes, attendance streaks.
💡 Example — Random word in practice

Problem: Design a better library experience.

Random word: "Airport"

Forced connections: Departure boards for books, lounge quiet zones, priority check-in for exams, a "flight" status update when your book is ready.

🧠 De Bono's lateral thinking
De Bono introduced Random Entry in his 1970 book Lateral Thinking. It's since been used by advertising agencies, R&D labs, and product teams to break through creative blocks.
Random inputs produce non-random breakthroughs when you force the connection.
✅ Tip

Use a physical random word generator — a dictionary, a word-of-the-day app, a stack of noun cards. Screens tend to bias toward recent searches.

⚠️ Watch out

Random Entry is not for every problem. Use it when the team is stuck and producing the same ideas repeatedly.

§ B-III.7

Design Heuristics Cards Extra

ToolK37 min

Design Heuristics is a set of 77 research-backed prompt cards — each one a design move that reliably generates new concepts. It's used in engineering design courses and product innovation workshops.

📘 Definition

A deck of 77 cards, each describing a distinct design heuristic (e.g., "Make it portable", "Add a sensor", "Use natural materials"). Teams draw a card and apply it to their problem.

Sample heuristics

  • Adjust to fit — can it adapt to different users or contexts?
  • Add a sensor — can technology feel the environment?
  • Combine — merge two functions into one object.
  • Make it portable — collapse it, shrink it, fold it.
  • Simplify — remove every non-essential part.
  • Use natural materials — wood, bamboo, leather.
  • Build in feedback — show the user the effect of their action.
💡 Example — Designing a student desk

Draw a card: "Add a sensor". Idea — a desk that dims its lamp when the user's face is too close, encouraging better posture.

Draw another: "Make it portable". Idea — a foldable version that becomes a carry-case.

🎓 Research origin — University of Michigan
The Design Heuristics card set was developed by researchers at the University of Michigan and Iowa State University after studying thousands of design ideas. They found that the strongest ideas came from the same 77 cognitive moves — and made them into a training tool.
The best designers don't have more ideas — they have more moves.
✅ Tip

Draw 3–5 cards at random, apply each to your problem for 5 minutes. Randomness plus structure is more productive than either alone.

⚠️ Watch out

Heuristics are prompts, not solutions. Applying them mechanically produces mechanical ideas. The value is in the interpretation.
