Unit IV — Prototyping, Testing & Validation

Seven methods professional design teams use to test, measure, and refine prototypes at scale.

§ B-IV.1

Wizard of Oz Prototyping Extra

MethodK37 min

Wizard of Oz prototyping simulates a finished product using a human hidden "behind the curtain". Users think they're interacting with the real thing — but a person is doing the work manually.

📘 Definition

A prototyping technique where the front-end looks real to the user, but the back-end is manually operated by a designer. Tests the experience before any complex technology is built.

How it works

  1. Build only the interface the user sees.
  2. Have a team member hidden with a laptop and a script.
  3. When the user types a query or presses a button, the operator manually responds.
  4. Users believe they're using a working AI or automated system.
  5. Observe their behaviour, emotions, and expectations.
💡 Example — Voice assistant test

Before building any NLP, a team sets up a booth. Users speak into a mic. Behind the wall, a person types the response and it plays through a speaker. In 2 hours, the team learns what users expect to be able to say — before spending months on the AI.

🗣️ IBM · Speech-to-text
IBM's early speech-recognition demos used exactly this technique. A human typist transcribed speech in real time. The demo "proved" the concept to investors before the technology was ready.
The feeling of a product can be tested before the product exists.
🚗 Ather Energy · Early demos
Early electric-scooter startups simulated dashboard behaviour using tethered phones before building real firmware. Users rode prototypes with a hidden laptop responding to throttle inputs.
Simulate the experience, then engineer it.
✅ Tip

Use Wizard of Oz when the front-end experience is more uncertain than the back-end technology. If the risk is the tech, this won't help.

⚠️ Watch out

Never deceive users permanently. Disclose the simulation after the test — it's an ethical requirement.

§ B-IV.2

Bodystorming Extra

MethodK26 min

Bodystorming is role-play prototyping where the team physically acts out the user experience in the real environment — with real props, real spaces, and real movement.

📘 Definition

A physical role-play technique where participants act out a service or product experience in a real or simulated environment to uncover friction points that paper prototyping misses.

How it differs from role-play

Role-PlayBodystorming
Seated, imagined scenarioStanding, in the real space
Verbal dialoguePhysical movement + props
Fast, casualStructured, immersive
Tests conversation flowTests physical flow and space
💡 Example — Hospital reception

A team stands in an actual hospital lobby. One plays a patient arriving; another plays the receptionist; a third plays a nurse. They move through the space exactly as a real patient would — walking to the desk, waiting, being redirected, sitting, being called. They discover that the queue is invisible from the door, and the signage is behind the patient. No amount of paper sketching reveals this.

🏥 IDEO · Healthcare design
IDEO famously bodystormed hospital services by sending designers to stand in queues, sit in waiting rooms, and walk corridors. Their redesigns often came from discovering spatial problems the client had never noticed.
Space is a material. Feel it to design it.
✅ Tip

Videotape bodystorming sessions. Small movements and hesitations are easy to miss live but obvious on replay.

⚠️ Watch out

Bodystorming feels awkward at first. Give participants permission to be silly — the awkwardness disappears after 2 minutes.

§ B-IV.3

A/B Testing Extra

MethodK37 min

A/B testing (or split testing) shows two versions of a design to two groups of users and measures which performs better. It replaces opinion with evidence.

📘 Definition

A controlled experiment where users are randomly split between version A and version B, and a defined metric (clicks, conversions, time) determines the winner.

How to run a valid A/B test

  1. Pick a single metric — "sign-up rate", not "engagement".
  2. Change one variable between A and B — button colour, headline, layout.
  3. Randomly assign users — no cherry-picking.
  4. Run long enough to reach statistical significance (usually ~1000 users per variant).
  5. Analyse. Adopt the winner. Log the learning.
💡 Example — Button colour test

A campus app tests "Sign Up" button colours — blue (A) and orange (B) — on 2000 visitors. Variant B's conversion is 12% vs A's 9%. Statistically significant → adopt orange, log the finding.

🔵 Google · 41 shades of blue
Google famously tested 41 shades of blue on search-result link colours. The winning shade generated an estimated $200 million in additional ad revenue per year. The single-pixel difference mattered — because the scale was huge.
Small design decisions have outsized impact at scale.
📧 Obama 2008 campaign
The Obama campaign A/B-tested landing page designs for donations. The winning variant raised an additional $60 million over the losing variant.
A/B testing isn't only for tech companies.
✅ Tip

Combine A/B testing with qualitative research. Numbers tell you what happened; interviews tell you why.

⚠️ Watch out

Stop worrying about "peeking" too early — but do stop before you have statistical significance. Small sample sizes produce random winners.

§ B-IV.4

Usability Metrics (SUS, NPS) Extra

ToolK38 min

Usability metrics turn "it feels nice" into a number. Two of the most widely used are SUS and NPS.

📘 Definition

SUS (System Usability Scale) — a 10-question survey scored 0–100, measuring perceived usability. NPS (Net Promoter Score) — a single question ("How likely are you to recommend?") scored −100 to +100, measuring customer loyalty.

SUS — System Usability Scale

10 statements rated 1–5. Example:

  • "I think that I would like to use this system frequently."
  • "I found the system unnecessarily complex."
  • "I thought the system was easy to use."

Final score 0–100. Benchmarks:

ScoreGradeMeaning
85+A+Best-in-class
72–84BGood
68CAverage
50–67DBelow average
Below 50FPoor

NPS — Net Promoter Score

One question: "On a scale of 0–10, how likely are you to recommend this to a friend?"

  • 9–10: Promoters
  • 7–8: Passives
  • 0–6: Detractors

NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors. Range: −100 to +100.

🚗 Tesla · NPS ~97
Tesla has recorded NPS scores above 95 — some of the highest in the automotive industry. That single number became a marketing asset, quoted in investor calls and press releases.
A well-chosen metric can become a brand signal.
✅ Tip

Use SUS for your product against itself across versions. Use NPS for your product against competitors.

⚠️ Watch out

Metrics can be gamed. If you tie a bonus to NPS, teams start asking only happy customers. Always pair numbers with qualitative research.

§ B-IV.5

Heuristic Evaluation Extra

MethodK38 min

Heuristic Evaluation is a usability inspection method where experts review a design against a fixed list of principles — without needing real users.

📘 Definition

A method where 3–5 expert reviewers independently inspect a user interface against a list of usability heuristics, then combine findings to prioritise fixes.

Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics

  1. Visibility of system status — users know what's happening (loading, saved).
  2. Match between system and real world — use familiar language and concepts.
  3. User control and freedom — undo, redo, exit easily.
  4. Consistency and standards — same words and actions mean the same thing.
  5. Error prevention — prevent mistakes before they happen.
  6. Recognition rather than recall — don't make users memorise.
  7. Flexibility and efficiency — shortcuts for experts; clarity for beginners.
  8. Aesthetic and minimalist design — every extra element competes with the important ones.
  9. Help users recognise and recover from errors — plain-language errors, clear fix.
  10. Help and documentation — easy to search, focused on the task.
💡 Example — Evaluating a login form

An evaluator finds:

  • Heuristic 1: no loading spinner after tapping "Login" — user unsure if it worked.
  • Heuristic 5: password rules shown only after failure — should be shown before typing.
  • Heuristic 9: error says "Auth failed" — should say "Incorrect password. Try again or reset."

Result: 3 concrete fixes, found in 30 minutes, no users needed.

👨‍🔬 Jakob Nielsen · 1990
Nielsen developed the heuristics after analysing 249 usability problems. His research showed that 3–5 evaluators catch around 75% of usability issues — a huge return for a few hours of expert review.
Expert review is fast, cheap, and catches most problems.
✅ Tip

Have each evaluator work independently first. Group discussion before individual review contaminates the findings.

⚠️ Watch out

Heuristic evaluation finds problems, not solutions. It tells you what's wrong, not how to fix it — that requires design work.

§ B-IV.6

Cognitive Walkthrough Extra

MethodK36 min

Cognitive Walkthrough is a structured usability inspection where the reviewer steps through each action a user would take and asks a small set of questions at each step.

📘 Definition

A task-based expert review method that examines whether users can figure out, at each step, what to do next — without training.

The four questions at each step

  1. Will the user try to achieve the right effect?
  2. Will the user notice the correct action is available?
  3. Will the user associate the correct action with the desired effect?
  4. If the correct action is performed, will the user see that progress is being made?
💡 Example — Booking a train ticket

Task: "Book a train from Chennai to Bengaluru."

Step 1 — Enter source/destination:

  • Q1: Will they try to enter cities? Yes, obvious.
  • Q2: Is the "From" field visible? Yes.
  • Q3: Do they associate "From/To" with journey? Yes.
  • Q4: Do they see any confirmation? No — the app doesn't highlight the entered cities. Fix: show entered cities as chips.
🖥️ Lewis & Polson · 1990
Cognitive Walkthrough was developed at IBM by Clayton Lewis and Peter Polson to help non-designer developers assess usability. It remains one of the cheapest methods to catch first-time-user problems.
First-time-user problems are best caught by first-time-user thinking.
✅ Tip

Use Cognitive Walkthrough for new users — onboarding flows, first-time checkout, first launch. For expert users, other methods work better.

⚠️ Watch out

Reviewers must genuinely forget what they know about the product. Designers who built it find their own design obvious.

§ B-IV.7

Remote & Unmoderated Testing Extra

MethodK37 min

Remote and unmoderated testing let you run user tests without being in the room — at scale, across geographies, on the user's own devices.

📘 Definition

Remote testing — sessions run via video call with a facilitator. Unmoderated testing — sessions run automatically; users follow instructions on screen and record themselves.

Moderated vs unmoderated

Moderated (in-person)Unmoderated (remote)
5–8 users per round20–100+ users per round
Deep qualitative insightBroad behavioural data
Expensive (lab, travel)Cheap (~$10/user on platforms)
Weeks to organiseHours to organise
Facilitator can probeNo follow-up questions
Best for: exploring whyBest for: testing at scale
💡 Example — Landing page test

An app team wants to know if their new signup flow works for 100 users. Instead of running 100 moderated sessions over two months, they use a remote platform. 100 users complete the flow in 3 days. The team reviews videos and notes where users hesitate, in less than a week.

🔬 UserTesting, Maze, Lookback
Platforms like UserTesting, Maze, and Lookback built multi-billion-dollar businesses on remote testing. Product teams now routinely test with users in 5 countries without leaving the office.
Distance is no longer a constraint on user research.
✅ Tip

Write clear task prompts. Without a facilitator, ambiguity kills the session. Test prompts internally before sending them to users.

⚠️ Watch out

Unmoderated tests miss emotions. Users skip frustration silently. Always supplement with qualitative interviews.
