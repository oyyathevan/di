§ B-V.1

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations Extra

FrameworkK28 min

Everett Rogers studied how new ideas spread through societies. In 1962 he published Diffusion of Innovations — a model that explains why some products catch on and others don't, regardless of quality.

📘 Definition

A theory that describes how a new idea or technology spreads through a population over time, categorised into five adopter groups: Innovators, Early Adopters, Early Majority, Late Majority, and Laggards.

The five adopter groups

Group% of marketWho they are
Innovators2.5%Risk-takers; try anything new
Early Adopters13.5%Opinion leaders; adopt before mass market
Early Majority34%Pragmatists; adopt once benefits are proven
Late Majority34%Skeptics; adopt after it's the norm
Laggards16%Traditionalists; adopt only when forced

The "chasm"

Geoffrey Moore later added the concept of the chasm — a gap between Early Adopters and the Early Majority. Many products die here because the strategy that worked for early adopters doesn't work for pragmatists.

📱 iPhone · Crossing the chasm
In 2007, only tech enthusiasts bought the iPhone. By 2011, everyone had one. Apple navigated the chasm by shifting messaging from "feature-rich" (for techies) to "it just works" (for everyone).
Different adopter groups need different products and messages.
🛴 Electric scooters in India
Electric scooters (Ather, Ola, TVS iQube) started with Innovators (2018–2020), moved to Early Adopters (2021–2022), and crossed into the Early Majority in 2023–2024 as charging infrastructure grew and prices dropped. The same product could not have succeeded in 2018.
Timing is a variable, not a background condition.
✅ Tip

Before launching, ask: "Which adopter group am I designing for right now?" A product for Innovators fails with the Late Majority — and vice versa.

⚠️ Watch out

Roger's original percentages were based on US farming innovations in the 1950s. Modern digital products can spread faster and differently — some skip Early Adopters entirely.

§ B-V.2

Disruptive Innovation Theory Extra

FrameworkK38 min

Clayton Christensen's Disruptive Innovation Theory explains how small, "worse" products topple industry leaders — often by starting at the bottom of the market and improving upward.

📘 Definition

A theory describing how a new entrant with a simpler, cheaper, or more accessible product starts by serving overlooked customers, then moves upmarket until it displaces the incumbent.

Sustaining vs disruptive

Sustaining InnovationDisruptive Innovation
Improves what already existsRedefines the market
Serves the best existing customersServes overlooked or non-customers
Incumbents winEntrants win
Faster, better, more featuresSimpler, cheaper, more convenient

The classic pattern

  1. A small player offers a "worse" product at a lower price.
  2. Incumbents ignore it — it's not a threat to their best customers.
  3. The entrant improves incrementally.
  4. The entrant eventually matches the incumbent on the features that matter.
  5. Customers switch en masse. The incumbent is left with a shrinking high-end market.
🎬 Netflix vs Blockbuster
Blockbuster (which had 9,000 stores at its peak) dismissed Netflix's mail-order DVDs as a niche. Netflix was slower, had no late-night runs, and fewer new releases. But it was more convenient. When streaming arrived, Netflix was ready. Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in 2010.
Disruption starts on the low end, never the high end.
🏍️ Hero Honda vs Bajaj
In the 1990s, Bajaj focused on scooters. Hero Honda offered motorcycles — seen as "uncomfortable" and "for men". Over the next two decades, motorcycles took over the Indian two-wheeler market as fuel efficiency and road conditions changed.
What looks niche can become the whole market.
✅ Tip

Use this framework when analysing a startup pitch: "Is this sustaining innovation for existing customers, or disruptive innovation creating new ones?" Both can succeed — but they need different strategies.

⚠️ Watch out

Not all disruption stories fit the theory. Uber, for example, didn't start at the bottom of the taxi market — it started at the top (premium black cars). The theory is a lens, not a law.

§ B-V.3

Blue Ocean Strategy Extra

FrameworkK37 min

Most companies compete in bloody "red oceans" — crowded markets with shrinking margins. Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne) argues that the best move is to create a new market entirely.

📘 Definition

A strategy where a company creates uncontested market space — a "blue ocean" — by offering a fundamentally different value proposition rather than competing on the same terms as everyone else.

Red ocean vs blue ocean

Red OceanBlue Ocean
Compete in existing marketsCreate new market space
Beat the competitionMake competition irrelevant
Exploit existing demandCreate new demand
Value-cost trade-offBreak the trade-off
Low price or high valueLow price and high value
🎪 Cirque du Soleil
Traditional circuses competed on star performers and animal shows. Cirque du Soleil cut both — no animals, no stars — and added theatrical storytelling and original music. It attracted adults willing to pay opera prices, not just families buying cheap circus tickets. A new market was born.
Sometimes you win by removing features, not adding them.
💻 Zerodha · India
Indian brokerage was a red ocean of high commissions. Zerodha launched with ₹0 delivery trading, charged a flat ₹20 per intraday trade, and built the platform around self-service. It became India's largest broker — not by beating competitors at their game, but by changing the game entirely.
Blue ocean isn't about being cheaper — it's about being structurally different.
✅ Tip

Use the "Four Actions Framework" (Kim & Mauborgne) to design a blue ocean: Eliminate — what can be removed? Reduce — what can be lowered? Raise — what can be lifted? Create — what should be added?

⚠️ Watch out

Blue oceans don't stay blue. Every success attracts competitors. Zerodha's ₹20 flat fee is now matched by dozens of brokers. Blue oceans are temporary advantages, not permanent ones.

§ B-V.4

Lean Startup (Build–Measure–Learn) Extra

MethodK38 min

Eric Ries's Lean Startup is the operating system behind most modern startups. Instead of writing a 50-page business plan, you build, measure, and learn in rapid cycles.

📘 Definition

A methodology for developing new products by iterating quickly — building the smallest possible version, measuring how users respond, and learning whether to pivot or persevere.

The core loop

     ┌─────────┐
     │  BUILD  │──────┐
     └─────────┘      │
          ▲           ▼
     ┌─────────┐  ┌─────────┐
     │  LEARN  │◀─│ MEASURE │
     └─────────┘  └─────────┘

  Build the smallest version
  Measure how users actually behave
  Learn → pivot or persevere
  Repeat in days, not months

Core concepts

  • MVP — the smallest product that tests the biggest assumption.
  • Validated Learning — learning backed by data, not opinion.
  • Pivot — a structured change in strategy while keeping the vision.
  • Persevere — continue if the data supports the direction.
  • Innovation Accounting — metrics that measure progress before revenue.
📦 Dropbox · The video MVP
Drew Houston wanted to build cloud storage but wasn't sure people wanted it. Instead of building Dropbox, he built a 3-minute demo video and posted it on Hacker News. 75,000 people signed up overnight. The MVP was a video — and it validated demand without writing a line of the product.
Your MVP doesn't have to be a product — it has to test the riskiest assumption.
🏢 Zappos · The shoe photo experiment
Before building a shoe store, Nick Swinmurn took photos of shoes at local shops, posted them online, and bought them from the shop when someone ordered. He was testing the assumption "people will buy shoes online" — with almost no investment.
Manually simulate the product before automating it.
✅ Tip

Every cycle should answer one specific question. "Do people want this?" is different from "Will they pay for this?" — and each needs its own MVP.

⚠️ Watch out

Lean Startup is not "just ship fast". It's "test the riskiest assumption as cheaply as possible". Shipping without measurement is not Lean — it's guessing.

§ B-V.5

Product-Market Fit (PMF) Extra

ConceptK37 min

Product-Market Fit is the moment a product stops needing to be pushed and starts pulling users toward it. It's the single most important milestone in any startup.

📘 Definition

The degree to which a product satisfies strong market demand. A product has PMF when users are buying, using, and recommending it without heavy marketing push.

Signals of PMF

  • Users are disappointed when the product breaks.
  • Word-of-mouth grows faster than marketing spend.
  • Retention curves flatten (users stay, not churn).
  • Sales cycle shortens — deals close without much effort.
  • Support tickets shift from "how does this work" to "please add this feature".

Signals of NO PMF

  • Every sale needs a discount or a push.
  • Users churn after the first week.
  • You're building features no one asked for.
  • You can't decide "who is this for?" in one sentence.
  • Growth requires endless paid ads.

The Sean Ellis test

Ask users: "How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?"

  • Very disappointed — the customer needs the product.
  • Somewhat disappointed — nice to have, not essential.
  • Not disappointed — no fit.

If 40%+ say "very disappointed", you likely have PMF.

📸 Instagram · 2010
Instagram launched in October 2010 and gained 1 million users in 2 months, then 10 million in a year. No ad spend. It had PMF. Compare this to the hundreds of photo apps launched the same year that got nowhere — same feature set, no fit.
PMF feels like gravity — users pull in without being pushed.
✅ Tip

Before PMF, focus on learning. After PMF, focus on scaling. Mixing these up is the #1 startup mistake — scaling before fit burns cash; learning after fit wastes opportunity.

⚠️ Watch out

PMF is not permanent. Markets shift, competitors emerge, and yesterday's fit is today's churn. Netscape had PMF in 1995; by 2000 it was gone.

§ B-V.6

Go-to-Market (GTM) Strategy Extra

FrameworkK37 min

Even a great product fails if no one knows about it. A Go-to-Market (GTM) Strategy is the plan for how you'll reach, convince, and convert your first real customers.

📘 Definition

The plan describing who you'll sell to, how you'll reach them, what you'll charge, and what channels you'll use — to turn a product into a business.

Six elements of a GTM strategy

ElementQuestion
Target segmentWho exactly are the first customers?
Value propositionWhat do you promise them?
PricingFree, freemium, subscription, one-time?
ChannelsAds, SEO, community, partnerships, sales team?
MessagingWhat's the one-line pitch?
Success metricsWhat does winning look like in 90 days?
📚 Notion · Community-led growth
Notion's GTM strategy wasn't paid ads — it was users creating templates and sharing them on Twitter and Reddit. The company invested in community features, making each user a distributor. Templates became the growth engine.
Great GTM makes your users your salesforce.
🚗 Tesla · Direct-to-consumer
Traditional car companies sold through dealerships. Tesla went direct — no franchise network. This let Tesla control pricing, messaging, and the buying experience. It was a GTM decision, not a product decision, that shaped the entire company.
Sometimes the channel is the innovation.
✅ Tip

Pick one channel and dominate it before adding a second. Most startups fail because they spread thin across five channels and win in none.

⚠️ Watch out

GTM is not marketing. Marketing is one part. GTM includes pricing, sales motion, onboarding, and customer success. All of it decides whether the product lands.

§ B-V.7

Business Model Innovation Extra

FrameworkK48 min

Most innovation focuses on what a company sells. Business Model Innovation changes how it creates, delivers, and captures value — often with the same product.

📘 Definition

Redesigning the way a company operates to create and capture value — changing pricing, delivery, partnerships, or revenue structure, not the product itself.

Four ways to innovate a business model

TypeWhat changesExample
Revenue modelHow you chargeNetflix — subscription vs. pay-per-view
Delivery modelHow it reaches the userSpotify — streaming vs. downloads
Value chainWho does whatApple App Store — ecosystem vs. in-house
Customer segmentWho you serveNano — car for a segment that couldn't afford cars
🎵 Spotify vs iTunes
iTunes sold songs for $0.99 each. Spotify offered unlimited streaming for a flat monthly fee. Same content, same users, different business model. iTunes is now dead; Spotify dominates. The innovation wasn't technology — it was the revenue model.
The same product, priced and delivered differently, can win a market.
🚗 Uber vs Taxis
Taxis existed for a century. Uber didn't build a better car — it changed the business model: no meters, no cash, no fleet ownership, dynamic pricing, and drivers as independent contractors. Same service, entirely different structure.
Business model innovation can disrupt an industry without any new technology.
🚙 Tata Nano · Segment innovation
The Nano wasn't a better car — it was a car aimed at a segment that didn't exist in the market: two-wheeler families who wanted four wheels. The business model shift was "sell volume at a price point no one thought was possible".
A new segment is a new market.
✅ Tip

When your product isn't winning, ask: "Is the problem the product, or the business model around it?" Many teams rebuild the product when they should rebuild the model.

⚠️ Watch out

Business model innovation is harder than product innovation. It touches sales, finance, operations, legal, and culture. A great model change often needs a new CEO.
