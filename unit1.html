§ 1.1

Introduction to Design Thinking

ConceptK215 min

Design Thinking is a human-centered method to solve problems. It helps us understand people first, then create solutions that really work.

📘 Definition

Design Thinking is a step-by-step way to understand users, define problems, create ideas, make prototypes and test them.

Why it is useful

  • Focuses on real user needs.
  • Encourages creativity.
  • Accepts failure as a way to learn.
  • Helps solve complex problems.
💡 Example

Instead of designing a bottle for “everyone,” DT asks who will use it and what problems they face.

🧭
§ 1.2

Design Thinking Mindset and Principles

ConceptK212 min

Mindset

  • Empathy: Understand people’s feelings.
  • Optimism: Believe a better solution is possible.
  • Iteration: Improve step by step.
  • Collaboration: Work with others.
  • Curiosity: Ask questions.
  • Open-mindedness: Accept new ideas.
  • Learning from failure: Failure gives information.

Principles

  1. Human-centered: Start with people.
  2. Show, don’t tell: Make prototypes, not just talk.
  3. Radical collaboration: Work with different people.
  4. Prototype early: Make rough models quickly.
  5. Iterate: Test and improve again and again.
✅ Tip

A team designing a school bag does not guess. They watch students, ask questions, make a paper model, test it, and improve it.

§ 1.4

Human-Centered Design

ConceptK210 min

Human-Centered Design (HCD) means designing with the people who will use the product.

Steps

  1. Hear: Listen to users.
  2. Create: Make ideas and prototypes.
  3. Deliver: Give the final solution.
💡 Example

A hospital app should be designed with patients, doctors and nurses — not only with software engineers.

§ 1.8

Empathy and User Research

MethodK315 min

Empathy means understanding another person’s feelings and needs.

User research methods

  • Interviews
  • Observation
  • Surveys
  • Contextual inquiry
  • Focus groups

Steps for empathy

  1. Listen without judging.
  2. Ask open-ended questions.
  3. Watch body language.
  4. Record what you see and hear.
  5. Try to feel what they feel.
🛒 IDEO · Shopping Cart Redesign
IDEO's team observed real shoppers for days — filming how they picked items, parked carts, and loaded cars. The redesign came from watching, not from surveys.
Observation beats assumption — real behavior tells you more than questions do.
💡 Example

A user says, “I hate waiting in line.” Empathy helps us understand they feel frustrated, tired and powerless — not just “impatient.”

🎤
§ 1.9

Interviews, Observation, Ethics

MethodK315 min

Interviews

  • Ask open-ended questions.
  • Avoid yes/no questions.
  • Example: “Tell me about the last time you used this app.”

Observation

Watch what people do, not just what they say.

Contextual Inquiry

Observe and ask questions in the real place. Example: go to the kitchen and watch how a person cooks.

Ethics

  • Take informed consent.
  • Protect privacy.
  • Keep data confidential.
  • Do no harm.
  • Allow users to stop anytime.
⚠️ Warning

Before recording an interview, always say: “May I record this? You can say no.”

📗 2-Mark Questions

Q1 (2M)
Define Design Thinking.
Answer: Design Thinking is a human-centered, iterative method to solve problems. It involves understanding users, defining problems, generating ideas, prototyping, and testing solutions with real feedback.
Q2 (2M)
List the five stages of the Design Thinking process.
Answer: (1) Empathize, (2) Define, (3) Ideate, (4) Prototype, (5) Test. The process loops back whenever new learning happens.
Q3 (2M)
What is empathy in Design Thinking?
Answer: Empathy is the ability to understand another person's feelings and needs by seeing the world from their perspective. It is different from sympathy — it means feeling with, not feeling for.
Q4 (2M)
Define a persona.
Answer: A persona is a fictional character representing a group of users. It includes a name, age, goal, and problem, and helps designers make decisions from the user's perspective.
Q5 (2M)
What is a stakeholder?
Answer: A stakeholder is anyone affected by a product or service — not just the end user. Examples include buyers, managers, family members, regulators, and support staff.
Q6 (2M)
What is an empathy map?
Answer: An empathy map is a four-quadrant tool that organises user research into what the user Says, Thinks, Does, and Feels.
Q7 (2M)
Differentiate between explicit and latent needs.
Answer: Explicit needs are what the user directly says. Latent needs are hidden or unspoken — discovered through observation and asking "why." Latent needs often matter more for innovation.
Q8 (2M)
What is Human-Centered Design (HCD)?
Answer: HCD is an approach that designs with the people who will use the product. It follows three steps: Hear (listen to users), Create (build ideas and prototypes), and Deliver (provide the final solution).

📘 5-Mark Questions

Q1 (5M)
Explain the Design Thinking mindset with examples.
Answer: The Design Thinking mindset is a set of attitudes that guide how designers approach problems:
  1. Empathy: Understand users' feelings first. Example: watch students use a bag before designing one.
  2. Optimism: Believe a better solution is possible, even for hard problems.
  3. Iteration: Improve step by step through repeated testing.
  4. Collaboration: Work with people from different backgrounds.
  5. Curiosity and learning from failure: Ask "why" constantly; treat failure as data, not defeat.
Together, these mindsets help teams solve messy, real-world problems.
Q2 (5M)
Compare Design Thinking with conventional engineering problem-solving.
Answer: The two approaches differ in six key ways:
PointConventional EngineeringDesign Thinking
ProblemGiven clearlyDiscovered by understanding users
FocusTechnical solutionHuman need
ProcessLinearIterative
GoalOptimize one solutionExplore many solutions
User roleLimitedCentral
FailureAvoidedUsed for learning
Q3 (5M)
Explain the five stages of the Design Thinking process briefly.
Answer: Design Thinking proceeds through five connected stages:
  1. Empathize: Understand users through interviews, observation, and immersion.
  2. Define: State the problem clearly using insights from research.
  3. Ideate: Generate many possible solutions using brainstorming and creative techniques.
  4. Prototype: Build a rough, cheap version of the best ideas.
  5. Test: Try it with real users, collect feedback, and iterate.
The process is not linear — teams loop back to earlier stages whenever new learning happens.
Q4 (5M)
Explain the four quadrants of an empathy map with an example.
Answer: An empathy map captures four aspects of a user:
  1. Says: What the user says out loud. Example: "I don't have time."
  2. Thinks: What they may be thinking silently. Example: "I wish someone could help."
  3. Does: What they actually do. Example: Orders food online instead of cooking.
  4. Feels: What emotions they show. Example: Tired and guilty.
Filling all four quadrants forces the designer to look beyond words and understand the full user experience.
Q5 (5M)
Explain personas and user journey maps. Why are they useful?
Answer:

Persona: A fictional character representing a user group. Example: "Ravi, 65, wants to buy groceries online but finds the text too small."

User Journey Map: A visual timeline of the steps a user takes — Awareness → Search → Compare → Buy → Use → Support — with pain points marked at each step.

Why useful: (1) They make abstract users feel real, (2) Guide design decisions from the user's perspective, (3) Reveal pain points that would otherwise be missed, (4) Help teams align on who they are designing for.

Q6 (5M)
Explain the ethical considerations in user research.
Answer: Ethical research protects participants. Five key rules:
  1. Informed consent: Always ask permission before recording or quoting.
  2. Privacy: Do not share personal details without permission.
  3. Confidentiality: Store data securely; anonymise where needed.
  4. Do no harm: Avoid questions or settings that cause discomfort.
  5. Right to stop: Users can end the interview at any time.
Ethical research builds trust and produces more honest data.

📕 10-Mark Questions

Q1 (10M)
Discuss the five-stage Design Thinking process with a real-world example. Draw a diagram.
Answer: Design Thinking is a five-stage iterative process that helps teams solve human-centered problems. Each stage is described below with an example — designing a better campus canteen experience.
  1. Empathize: Interview students, observe queues at peak hours, note frustrations. Insight: students skip lunch because waits exceed 15 minutes.
  2. Define: Write a clear problem statement: "Second-year students need a fast way to order lunch because their break is only 20 minutes."
  3. Ideate: Brainstorm 20+ ideas — mobile pre-order, QR menus, express counters, WhatsApp bots. Use SCAMPER and decision matrix to pick the strongest.
  4. Prototype: Build a paper mock-up of a QR pre-order system. Cost: ₹0. Time: 1 hour.
  5. Test: Let 5 students try it. Feedback: "QR too small," "want saved favourites." Iterate to v2.
Diagram to draw: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test ↑ | |___________ Iterate back ________________|

Conclusion: The process is not linear. Testing reveals gaps that send the team back to earlier stages. This is why Design Thinking produces solutions that real people actually adopt.

Q2 (10M)
Explain Human-Centered Design (HCD). How does it differ from traditional design? Illustrate with an example.
Answer:

Definition: Human-Centered Design is an approach that puts the needs, feelings, and context of real users at the center of every design decision.

Three steps of HCD:

  1. Hear: Listen to users through interviews, observation, and immersion.
  2. Create: Turn insights into prototypes through collaborative workshops.
  3. Deliver: Refine and launch solutions, continuously testing with users.

How it differs from traditional design:

Traditional DesignHuman-Centered Design
Designer decidesUser informs every decision
Starts with a briefStarts with user research
Tests at the endTests throughout
One-off deliveryIterative improvement

Example: A hospital app built with traditional design might focus on features. Built with HCD, the team interviews patients, nurses, and doctors, then designs around their real workflows — like one-tap appointment booking for elderly patients.

Conclusion: HCD produces solutions that fit real life, not just the designer's imagination.

Q3 (10M)
Discuss user research methods used in Design Thinking. Include observation, interviews, contextual inquiry, and ethics.
Answer: User research is the foundation of Design Thinking. It reveals what people actually need — not what they say they need.
  1. Interviews: Ask open-ended questions ("Tell me about the last time…") to uncover stories. Avoid yes/no questions.
  2. Observation: Watch users in real situations. What people do often differs from what they say.
  3. Contextual Inquiry: Combine observation and interview in the user's environment — kitchen, classroom, shop — to see real constraints.
  4. Surveys: Collect data from many users quickly, useful for validating patterns.
  5. Focus groups: Group discussions reveal shared concerns and language.

Ethical rules: Take informed consent, protect privacy, keep data confidential, do no harm, allow users to stop anytime.

Example: Nokia researchers lived with users in Indian villages. They discovered phones were shared between family members and charged at shops — insights that reshaped phone design globally.

Conclusion: Good user research combines methods and respects ethics. It transforms raw observation into actionable insight.

Q4 (10M)
Explain the process of synthesising user research. How do observations become insights and opportunities? Give an example.
Answer: Synthesis is the process of turning messy raw research into clear insights and opportunities.

Five-step process:

  1. Collect all notes: Gather every interview, observation, and quote.
  2. Group similar notes: Cluster related observations together (affinity mapping).
  3. Find patterns: Look for themes that repeat across users.
  4. Write insights: Turn each pattern into a deeper understanding of why users behave that way.
  5. Create opportunity statements: Frame insights into "How might we…" directions.

Observations vs Insights:

  • Observation = what happened ("Students skip breakfast").
  • Insight = why it happened ("They lack time, not money").
  • Opportunity = what to do about it ("Design a quick, healthy breakfast pack").

Example: A team observes that bus commuters miss breakfast. Grouping notes reveals the pattern: everyone rushes in the morning. Insight: the real pain is time, not hunger. Opportunity: a pre-packaged breakfast sold at bus stops.

Conclusion: Synthesis is where raw research becomes strategy. Without it, teams drown in data. With it, they find focus.
