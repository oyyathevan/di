Notes grouped into themes → insights emerge

Steps

  1. Write each observation on a sticky note.
  2. Put similar notes together.
  3. Give each group a name (theme).
  4. Find insights from groups.
🏠 Airbnb · Early Research
Airbnb's founders traveled to New York, met hosts, took photos, and wrote every observation on a wall. The clustered notes revealed a single insight: "Photos drive bookings."
Affinity mapping turns a wall of sticky notes into one clear, actionable insight.
💡 Example

Notes: [A] long queue, [B] confusing menu, [C] slow payment, [D] queue at 12:45, [E] cash only.

Group 1: A + D → Time problem

Group 2: B → Clarity problem

Group 3: C + E → Payment friction

§ 2.2

Patterns, Pain Points & Problem Statements

ConceptK310 min
  • Pattern: Something that repeats across users.
  • Pain point: A difficulty or frustration.
📘 Format

Problem Statement: [User] needs [need] because [insight].

💡 Example

Busy office workers need a quick lunch option because they have only 20 minutes to eat.

✅ Tip

Good problem statements are narrow. "Help everyone eat better" is too broad. "Help office workers find fast, cheap lunch" is workable.

§ 2.3

POV Statements & HMW Questions

MethodK312 min
From POV to HMW
POV User + Need + Insight
→
HMW How might we…?
One POV → many HMW questions → many solutions

POV Statement

📘 Format

[User] needs [need] because [surprising insight].

💡 Example

A new student needs a simple campus map because the current app is confusing.

HMW Questions

Turn POV into a question starting with "How might we…?"

💡 Example

How might we help new students find their way easily?

🚗 Ford · "Faster Horses"
Henry Ford reportedly said: "If I had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses." His POV was deeper: people didn't want horses — they wanted faster, easier travel.
POV looks past the literal request to the real underlying need.

5 Whys — Root Cause Staircase

Descending the Why Chain
Problem
  │
  ▼  Why? ────────  Truck left late
     │
     ▼  Why? ─────  Loading took long
        │
        ▼  Why? ──  Packages not sorted
           │
           ▼  Why?  No sorting system
              │
              ▼  🎯 Root cause: No training
🏭 Toyota · Origin of 5 Whys
Sakichi Toyoda invented the 5 Whys on the factory floor. When a machine stopped, workers asked "why" five times — reaching root causes instead of patching symptoms.
Fixing symptoms is temporary. Fixing root causes is permanent.
💡 Example

Problem: Customer order is late.

  1. Why? Delivery truck left late.
  2. Why? Loading took long.
  3. Why? Packages not sorted.
  4. Why? No sorting system.
  5. Why? No training.

Root cause: Lack of training.

§ 2.6

Problem Validation, Constraints & Specifications

MethodK312 min

Problem Validation

Check if the problem is real.

  • Talk to users.
  • Collect data.
  • See if many people face it.

Design Constraints

Limits like time, money, materials, laws, technology.

Specifications

Measurable requirements.

  • "Bottle must hold 1 litre."
  • "App must load in 2 seconds."
  • "Must cost less than ₹500."
🍽 Zomato · Early Validation
Before building the app, Zomato's founders printed restaurant menus into a physical booklet and tested demand at offices. Only after seeing real pull did they invest in the digital product.
Validate the problem cheaply before building an expensive solution.
⚠️ Warning

Don't confuse a constraint (limit) with a specification (target). "Budget under ₹1000" is a constraint; "must hold 1 litre" is a spec.

📗 2-Mark Questions

Q1 (2M)
Define affinity mapping.
Answer: Affinity mapping is a technique that groups similar observations into themes to reveal patterns and generate insights from raw research data.
Q2 (2M)
What is a pain point?
Answer: A pain point is a specific difficulty or frustration a user faces. It is discovered through interviews, observation, and journey maps.
Q3 (2M)
What is a POV statement?
Answer: A Point-of-View statement combines the user, their need, and a surprising insight into one sentence. Format: [User] needs [need] because [insight].
Q4 (2M)
Define the 5 Whys technique.
Answer: The 5 Whys is a root-cause analysis technique where "Why?" is asked five times to reach the underlying cause of a problem. It was developed by Sakichi Toyoda at Toyota.
Q5 (2M)
What is a Quick Win in the need-opportunity matrix?
Answer: A Quick Win is a problem-opportunity pair where both user need and innovation opportunity are high. It is the best combination to start with — high impact and high feasibility.
Q6 (2M)
Distinguish between a design constraint and a specification.
Answer: A constraint is a limit that shapes the solution (e.g., budget under ₹1000, laws, materials). A specification is a measurable requirement the solution must meet (e.g., "must hold 1 litre").
Q7 (2M)
What is the Triple Bottom Line?
Answer: The Triple Bottom Line is the three-pillar sustainability framework: People (social impact), Planet (environmental impact), and Profit (economic impact). Every solution should balance all three.
Q8 (2M)
How is a problem selected from multiple candidates?
Answer: A problem is selected by checking four criteria: importance, solvability, availability of resources, and potential impact on many users. The best candidates have high impact and high feasibility.

📘 5-Mark Questions

Q1 (5M)
Explain affinity mapping and how observations become insights.
Answer: Affinity mapping is a synthesis technique that turns raw research into clear insights.
  1. Collect observations: Every interview, quote, and observation on a sticky note.
  2. Cluster: Similar notes are grouped together by theme.
  3. Name themes: Each cluster gets a title (e.g., "Time pressure", "Payment friction").
  4. Find patterns: Look for themes that repeat across users.
  5. Write insights: Convert patterns into deeper "why" statements.
Example: Observation "students skip breakfast" + pattern across users → Insight "they lack time, not money."
Q2 (5M)
Explain POV statements and HMW questions with examples.
Answer:

POV Statement reframes a problem from the user's perspective. Format: [User] needs [need] because [surprising insight].

Example: "A new student needs a simple campus map because the current app is confusing."

HMW Question converts a POV into an open-ended ideation prompt. Format: "How might we…?"

Example: "How might we help new students find their way easily?"

Benefits: (1) Opens many solutions instead of one, (2) Avoids blame, (3) Focuses team effort, (4) Encourages optimism.

Q3 (5M)
Explain the 5 Whys technique with an example.
Answer: The 5 Whys is a root-cause analysis technique developed by Sakichi Toyoda at Toyota. The idea is to keep asking "Why?" until the underlying cause is found.

Example — late deliveries:

  1. Why is delivery late? Truck left late.
  2. Why did the truck leave late? Loading took long.
  3. Why did loading take long? Packages were not sorted.
  4. Why weren't they sorted? No sorting system.
  5. Why no sorting system? No training provided.

Root cause: Lack of training. Fixing symptoms would fail; fixing training prevents recurrence.

Q4 (5M)
Explain the need-opportunity matrix with examples.
Answer: The need-opportunity matrix plots problems on two axes: user need (low/high) and innovation opportunity (low/high). Four quadrants result:
  1. Quick Wins: High need + High opportunity → start here.
  2. Big Bets: High need + Low opportunity → worth it later with more resources.
  3. Fill-ins: Low need + High opportunity → easy but low impact.
  4. Avoid: Low need + Low opportunity → not worth pursuing.

Example: For a campus canteen app — long queues (high need, high opportunity) is a Quick Win; a drone food delivery system is a Big Bet.

Q5 (5M)
Explain problem validation, constraints and specifications.
Answer:

Problem validation: Check if the problem is real by talking to users, collecting data, and confirming that many people face it. Unvalidated problems waste design effort.

Constraints: Limits that shape the solution — time, money, materials, laws, technology. They narrow the design space.

Specifications: Measurable requirements the solution must meet. Example: "Bottle must hold 1 litre" or "App must load in 2 seconds."

Difference: Constraints restrict (what you can't do); specifications define targets (what you must achieve). Both must be documented before design begins.

Q6 (5M)
Explain social, environmental and sustainability considerations in Design Thinking.
Answer: Design Thinking must consider the Triple Bottom Line — People, Planet, Profit:
  1. People (Social): Does the solution help all users, including vulnerable groups?
  2. Planet (Environmental): Does it reduce waste, energy use, or pollution?
  3. Profit (Economic): Is it financially sustainable?

Example: Patagonia's Worn Wear program repairs old jackets — lower profit margin, but lower environmental impact and stronger customer loyalty.

Sources of innovation opportunities: Unmet needs, new technology, changing trends, government policies, social problems.

📕 10-Mark Questions

Q1 (10M)
Discuss the process of synthesis in Design Thinking. How do observations become insights and opportunities? Illustrate.
Answer: Synthesis is the process of turning messy raw research into clear, actionable insights.

Five-step process:

  1. Collect all notes: Gather every interview, observation, and quote.
  2. Group similar notes (affinity mapping): Cluster related observations into themes.
  3. Find patterns: Look for themes that repeat across users.
  4. Write insights: Convert each pattern into a deeper understanding of why.
  5. Create opportunity statements: Frame insights as "How might we…" directions.

Observation vs Insight vs Opportunity:

  • Observation — what happened (students skip breakfast).
  • Insight — why it happened (they lack time, not money).
  • Opportunity — what to do about it (design a quick, healthy breakfast pack).

Real-world example: Airbnb founders clustered host interviews and photos on a wall. The insight — "Photos drive bookings" — became the opportunity to launch professional photography for hosts.

Diagram to draw: Research Notes → Affinity Map → Patterns → Insights → Opportunities → HMW (cluster) (repeat) (why) (what)

Conclusion: Synthesis is where raw data becomes strategy. Skipping it leaves teams drowning in information.

Q2 (10M)
Explain stakeholder mapping, 5 Whys and problem prioritization. How do they help in defining a problem?
Answer:

1. Stakeholder Mapping: A 2×2 grid classifying people by influence (high/low) and interest (high/low). Four quadrants: Manage Closely (high + high), Keep Satisfied (high influence + low interest), Keep Informed (low influence + high interest), Monitor (low + low).

2. 5 Whys: Ask "Why?" five times to reach the root cause. Example — late deliveries → truck left late → loading slow → packages unsorted → no system → no training. Root cause: no training.

3. Problem Prioritization: Rank candidate problems by impact, urgency, feasibility, and cost. Then plot on the need-opportunity matrix to pick Quick Wins first.

How they help:

  • Stakeholder mapping ensures no affected group is ignored.
  • 5 Whys prevents solving symptoms instead of root causes.
  • Prioritization prevents wasting effort on low-impact problems.

Conclusion: Together, they convert a vague problem area into a well-defined, high-impact design challenge.

Q3 (10M)
Discuss POV statements and HMW questions with real-world examples. Why are they important in Design Thinking?
Answer:

POV Statement: A single sentence that combines user, need, and surprising insight. Format: [User] needs [need] because [surprising insight].

Examples:

  • "A new student needs a simple campus map because the current app is confusing."
  • "A busy mother needs quick meal options because she has no time to cook."

HMW Questions: Convert POV into an open-ended prompt starting with "How might we…?"

  • "How might we help new students find their way easily?"
  • "How might we make meal preparation fast for busy mothers?"

Importance:

  1. POV sharpens the problem from the user's view — not the designer's.
  2. POV avoids the "faster horse" trap by looking past the literal request.
  3. HMW opens many solutions instead of locking into one.
  4. HMW is optimistic — it assumes a solution exists.
  5. Together, they act as a bridge between research and ideation.
Diagram to draw: Research → POV (user + need + insight) → HMW questions → Ideation (one) (many)

Conclusion: A good POV and well-formed HMW questions set the direction for the entire ideation phase.

Q4 (10M)
Discuss problem validation, constraints, sustainability and problem selection in Design Thinking. Give a full example.
Answer:

1. Problem Validation: Verify the problem is real — talk to users, collect data, confirm multiple users face it. Unvalidated problems waste time.

2. Constraints and Specifications:

  • Constraints — limits: budget ₹5000, deadline 4 weeks, laws, technology.
  • Specifications — measurable targets: must load in 2s, must weigh under 1kg.

3. Social, Environmental & Sustainability: Apply the Triple Bottom Line — People (social benefit), Planet (environmental impact), Profit (financial viability).

4. Problem Selection: Choose one problem using four criteria: importance, solvability, resources, impact. Use the need-opportunity matrix to pick Quick Wins.

Full example — Campus canteen problem:

  1. Validation: 80% of students confirm long queues at 12:45.
  2. Constraints: ₹0 budget, 3-week deadline, must work on basic phones.
  3. Specs: order placed in under 60 seconds; ready in under 10 minutes.
  4. Sustainability: reduces paper waste (digital menu), no extra staffing cost (People + Planet + Profit all positive).
  5. Selection: high user need + high opportunity = Quick Win.

Conclusion: Validated, well-constrained, sustainable problems are the foundation for successful innovation.
