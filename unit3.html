TRIZ Basics & Individual / Collaborative Ideation

MethodK312 min

TRIZ is a method for inventive problem solving.

Basic principles

  • Segmentation
  • Separation
  • Local quality
  • Asymmetry
  • Merging
  • Universality
  • Nesting
  • Counterweight
  • Prior action
  • Feedback
💡 Example — Segmentation

Make a sofa into separate modules — easier to move, customise, and repair.

📱 Samsung · Battery Cooling
Samsung engineers used TRIZ principles to solve a battery overheating problem — moving from "more cooling" to "separating heat sources." Classic TRIZ contradiction resolution.
TRIZ looks at the conflict, not just the solution.

Ideation Modes

  • Individual: Work alone quietly — deeper focus, no groupthink.
  • Collaborative: Work in a team — more ideas, faster feedback.

§ 3.6

AI-Assisted Ideation

ToolK310 min

Opportunities

  • Fast idea generation.
  • Many variations in seconds.
  • Helps when stuck.
  • Combines ideas from unrelated fields.

Limitations

  • AI can be biased by its training data.
  • AI may not understand your real user context.
  • AI ideas need human judgment.
  • AI can't feel empathy — only you can.
💡 Example prompt

"Give 20 ideas for a low-cost water filter for rural India." Then evaluate each idea with real user criteria — cost, maintenance, culture.

💻 GitHub Copilot · Adobe Firefly
These tools help professionals brainstorm code and visual designs at 10× speed — but the final creative direction is still decided by the human. AI is the assistant, not the author.
Use AI to widen the funnel, not to replace judgment.

📗 2-Mark Questions

Q1 (2M)
Differentiate between divergent and convergent thinking.
Answer: Divergent thinking generates many ideas without judgment (quantity). Convergent thinking selects the best idea using criteria (quality). Both are needed — diverge first, converge later.
Q2 (2M)
Expand SCAMPER and list the seven lenses.
Answer: SCAMPER: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse. It is a guided ideation checklist that helps redesign an existing product or idea.
Q3 (2M)
Define biomimicry with an example.
Answer: Biomimicry is copying nature's designs to solve human problems. Example: Velcro was inspired by burrs sticking to dog fur; the Shinkansen bullet train nose was inspired by the kingfisher's beak.
Q4 (2M)
What is lateral thinking?
Answer: Lateral thinking solves problems sideways rather than head-on, ignoring the obvious path. Example: instead of making a better mousetrap, design a mouse repellent.
Q5 (2M)
What is TRIZ?
Answer: TRIZ is an inventive problem-solving method based on 40 principles. It focuses on resolving contradictions rather than accepting trade-offs.
Q6 (2M)
What is AI-assisted ideation? Mention one benefit and one limitation.
Answer: AI-assisted ideation uses AI tools (like ChatGPT) to generate many ideas quickly. Benefit: fast, wide idea generation. Limitation: AI may be biased and lacks real user context.
Q7 (2M)
What is the impact-feasibility matrix?
Answer: A 2×2 matrix classifying ideas by impact and feasibility into four quadrants: Quick Wins (high/high), Big Bets (high impact, low feasibility), Fill-ins (low impact, high feasibility), Avoid (low/low).
Q8 (2M)
What is a decision matrix? Why are weights important?
Answer: A decision matrix is a weighted table that scores concepts across multiple criteria. Weights show how important each criterion is — changing a weight can change the winning concept.

📘 5-Mark Questions

Q1 (5M)
Explain the rules of brainstorming and brainwriting. How does brainwriting improve on brainstorming?
Answer:

Brainstorming rules:

  1. No criticism — ideas flow freely.
  2. Wild ideas are welcome — they spark other ideas.
  3. Build on others' ideas.
  4. Quantity over quality — the more ideas, the better.

Brainwriting: Ideas are written silently, then passed to another person who builds on them.

How brainwriting improves on brainstorming:

  • Removes shyness — quieter members contribute.
  • Avoids groupthink — everyone writes independently.
  • Produces more ideas in the same time.
  • Prevents domination by loud voices.
Q2 (5M)
Explain SCAMPER with a suitable example.
Answer: SCAMPER is a guided ideation checklist with seven lenses:
  1. Substitute: Replace a part. (Backpack — use recycled plastic.)
  2. Combine: Merge two ideas. (Backpack + solar charger.)
  3. Adapt: Borrow from elsewhere. (Backpack — waterproof like a diver's bag.)
  4. Modify: Change size or shape. (Add more pockets.)
  5. Put to another use: (Use the bag as a seat.)
  6. Eliminate: Remove parts. (Remove extra straps.)
  7. Reverse: Flip the design. (Make it inside-out.)

Real example: McDonald's used Substitute (beef → potato) to create the McAloo Tikki for India.

Q3 (5M)
Explain biomimicry with two real-world examples.
Answer: Biomimicry copies nature's designs to solve human problems. Nature has solved engineering problems over millions of years of evolution.

Example 1 — Shinkansen Bullet Train: The train's tunnel boom was solved by copying the kingfisher bird's beak. This reduced noise and cut energy use by 15%.

Example 2 — Velcro: George de Mestral invented Velcro after observing burrs sticking to his dog's fur. The microscopic hooks inspired the hook-and-loop fastener.

Why biomimicry works: Nature's designs are tested, efficient, and sustainable. They often give us ready-made solutions we'd never invent from scratch.

Q4 (5M)
Explain morphological analysis with an example.
Answer: Morphological analysis breaks a problem into independent parts, lists options for each part, then recombines them to create new solutions.

Steps:

  1. Break the product into parts (e.g., chair: material, shape, price).
  2. List 3–5 options per part.
  3. Randomly combine one option from each list.
  4. Evaluate the resulting concepts.

Example — designing a chair:

  • Material: Plastic | Metal | Bamboo
  • Shape: Round | Square | Oval
  • Price: Low | Medium | High

3 × 3 × 3 = 27 possible combinations. Most are ordinary — a few are novel (e.g., bamboo + oval + medium).

Q5 (5M)
Explain TRIZ and its basic principles with an example.
Answer: TRIZ (Theory of Inventive Problem Solving) is a method developed by Genrich Altshuller. It focuses on resolving contradictions rather than accepting trade-offs.

Basic principles:

  • Segmentation: Split into independent parts.
  • Separation: Separate conflicting requirements.
  • Local quality: Make each part optimal for its function.
  • Merging: Combine similar functions.
  • Universality: One part, many uses.
  • Nesting: Place one object inside another.
  • Prior action: Do something before it's needed.
  • Feedback: Use output to adjust input.

Example: Samsung solved battery overheating by separating heat sources instead of adding more cooling — resolving the contradiction that more cooling needed more space.

Q6 (5M)
Explain the decision matrix with an example.
Answer: A decision matrix is a weighted scoring table for comparing concepts across multiple criteria.

Steps:

  1. List criteria (e.g., cost, usability, safety).
  2. Assign a weight to each criterion — total = 100%.
  3. Score each concept on a scale (1–5) for each criterion.
  4. Multiply score × weight, sum the totals.
  5. Highest total wins.

Example:

CriteriaWeightABC
Cost30%354
Usability40%534
Safety30%445
Total100%4.13.94.3

Concept C wins with 4.3. If Cost weight rises to 60%, Concept B wins instead.

📕 10-Mark Questions

Q1 (10M)
Discuss creativity mindset, divergent and convergent thinking, and brainstorming methods with examples.
Answer:

Creativity mindset: Believing that new and useful ideas can be generated through process, not just talent. Innovation = idea + implementation + value.

Divergent Thinking: Generate many ideas without judgment. Example: list 50 uses for a bottle — question, decorate, float, store coins.

Convergent Thinking: Select the best idea using criteria. Example: choose the bottle idea that's cheapest, most useful, and safest.

Brainstorming: A group method with four rules — no criticism, wild ideas welcome, build on others, quantity over quality.

Brainwriting: Silent version — reduces shyness and groupthink.

SCAMPER: A 7-lens checklist (Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to use, Eliminate, Reverse) that forces guided ideation. Example: redesign a backpack using each lens.

Mind Mapping: A central idea with radiating branches — visually shows connections between ideas.

Diagram to draw: Problem → DIVERGE (many ideas) → CONVERGE (criteria) → Best idea (brainstorm, brainwriting, SCAMPER, mind map)

Conclusion: Combining divergent and convergent thinking with structured tools produces both creativity and quality — the foundation of Design Thinking ideation.

Q2 (10M)
Discuss biomimicry, analogical thinking and lateral thinking with real-world examples.
Answer:

1. Biomimicry: Copying nature's designs. Examples: Shinkansen nose (kingfisher beak) reduced noise and energy by 15%; Velcro invented from burrs on dog fur.

2. Analogical Thinking: Borrowing solutions from unrelated fields. Example: airport security queues redesigned using the fast-food drive-through model — split into two parallel streams, one for bags, one for people.

3. Lateral Thinking: Solving sideways instead of head-on.

  • Instead of better mousetrap → mouse repellent.
  • Instead of faster trains → redesign the nose to reduce drag.

Reverse Brainstorming: "How do we make the problem worse?" then reverse. Example: to improve a queue, list what makes queues terrible (more steps, slow cashier) then reverse each.

Why these methods work: They bypass mental blocks. Direct thinking leads to obvious answers; sideways methods lead to novel ones.

Diagram to draw: Nature ────→ Biomimicry ────→ Human solution Other field → Analogical ────→ Borrowed idea Sideways ────→ Lateral ────→ Unexpected answer

Conclusion: The best ideas often come from outside our own field — nature, other industries, or deliberately breaking the rules.

Q3 (10M)
Discuss TRIZ, individual and collaborative ideation, and AI-assisted ideation with opportunities and limitations.
Answer:

1. TRIZ: A structured method for inventive problem-solving. It uses 40 principles (segmentation, merging, nesting, feedback) to resolve contradictions. Example: instead of adding more cooling to an overheating battery, separate the heat sources — Samsung used this approach.

2. Individual Ideation: Working alone gives deep focus and avoids groupthink. Best for complex problems needing concentration.

3. Collaborative Ideation: Working in a team produces more ideas, cross-pollinates perspectives, and gives fast feedback. Best for brainstorming at scale.

4. AI-Assisted Ideation: Using AI tools (ChatGPT, Midjourney) to generate ideas fast.

Opportunities: 10× speed, hundreds of variations, helps when stuck, combines ideas from unrelated fields.

Limitations: Biased by training data, no real user context, needs human judgment, cannot feel empathy.

Diagram to draw: TRIZ → contradiction resolution Individual ———— Team ———— AI (focus) (volume) (speed) ↓ ↓ ↓ All feed the same ideation pipeline

Conclusion: The best ideation combines individual focus, team collaboration, TRIZ structure, and AI speed — with human judgment as the final filter.

Q4 (10M)
Discuss the process of selecting the best idea — clustering, screening, impact-feasibility, and decision matrix.
Answer: After generating 20–50 ideas, they must be filtered systematically.

1. Idea Clustering: Group similar ideas into themes. Example: for a campus app — themes are "time-saving", "payment", "communication".

2. Screening: Remove ideas that are too expensive, not useful, or impossible. This cuts the list from 50 to ~10.

3. Impact-Feasibility Matrix: Plot remaining ideas in a 2×2 grid.

  • Quick Wins (high impact + high feasibility) — start here.
  • Big Bets (high impact + low feasibility) — later.
  • Fill-ins (low impact + high feasibility) — optional.
  • Avoid (low + low) — discard.

4. Decision Matrix: Score the top 3 concepts against weighted criteria (cost, usability, safety).

Example:

CriteriaWeightABC
Cost30%354
Usability40%534
Safety30%445
Total100%4.13.94.3

Concept C wins with 4.3.

Diagram to draw: 50 ideas → Cluster (5 themes) → Screen (10 remain) → Impact-Feasibility (3 remain) → Decision Matrix (1 winner)

Conclusion: Systematic selection removes personal bias and makes the final choice defensible in a design review.
