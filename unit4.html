Define a prototype.

List any four types of prototypes.

What is an MVP?
Answer: MVP stands for Minimum Viable Prototype — the smallest version that tests the core idea with real users. Example: Dropbox's demo video.
Q4 (2M)
Differentiate between low-fidelity and high-fidelity prototypes.
Answer: Low-fidelity = rough, cheap, quick (paper sketches). High-fidelity = close to final product (clickable Figma mock-up). Start low-fi, save high-fi for ideas that survive testing.
Q5 (2M)
What is the think-aloud method?
Answer: A user-testing method where users speak their thoughts while using the prototype. It reveals where they get stuck, not just that they get stuck.
Q6 (2M)
Define iterative design.
Answer: Iterative design is a repeated cycle: design → prototype → test → learn → redesign. Each cycle improves the solution based on real feedback.
Q7 (2M)
What is an iteration log?
Answer: A document recording every change made to a prototype — what changed, why, and what happened after. It shows progress and helps in final reporting.
Q8 (2M)
What is CAD? Give one example.
Answer: CAD = Computer-Aided Design — software used to create 3D models. Examples: Fusion 360, AutoCAD, SolidWorks.
Q9 (2M)
What is a service prototype?

📘 5-Mark Questions

Q1 (5M)
Explain the different types of prototypes with examples.
Answer: Prototypes vary in fidelity and medium:
  1. Paper prototype: Hand-drawn screens. Example: a sketch of a mobile app's home screen.
  2. Physical prototype: A 3D model built from cardboard, clay, or 3D printing. Example: a foam model of a new bottle shape.
  3. Digital prototype: Clickable mock-up in Figma or Adobe XD. Example: a working prototype of a food-ordering app.
  4. Storyboard: Comic-strip sequence of scenes. Example: 4 panels showing how a user books a cab.
  5. Role-play prototype: Team acts out the user experience. Example: acting out a hotel check-in.
Each type costs less than building the real product and reveals different kinds of problems.
Q2 (5M)
Explain the MVP concept with the Dropbox example.
Answer:

MVP (Minimum Viable Prototype): The smallest version of a solution that can test the core idea with real users. It answers the riskiest question — is this needed? — at the lowest possible cost.

Dropbox example: Before writing a single line of product code, Drew Houston made a 3-minute demo video showing how Dropbox would work. He posted it online. Over 75,000 people signed up overnight, proving demand without any actual product.

Why MVP works:

  • Tests the riskiest assumption first.
  • Saves money and time.
  • Learns from real user interest, not opinions.
  • Attracts early adopters and feedback.
Q3 (5M)
Explain user testing and the think-aloud method.
Answer:

User testing: Give the prototype to real users and watch them use it. The designer observes without helping — only notes what happens.

Think-aloud method: Users speak their thoughts while using the prototype. Example: "I'm looking for the search button… I can't find it." This reveals the user's mental model and where they get stuck.

Steps:

  1. Prepare tasks (e.g., "Book a cab to the airport").
  2. Ask user to perform tasks while thinking aloud.
  3. Observe and record — no helping.
  4. Ask questions after.
  5. Note problems and iterate.
Q4 (5M)
Explain iterative design and design critique.
Answer:

Iterative design: A repeated cycle — design → prototype → test → learn → redesign. Each cycle is better because it responds to real feedback.

Design critique: A structured peer-review session where the team answers:

  • What works?
  • What does not work?
  • What can be improved?

Example — Instagram: The original app "Burbn" had many features. User testing showed people only used photo-sharing. The team iterated radically, cut everything else, and Instagram was born.

Why both matter: Critique catches issues early; iteration fixes them systematically.

Q5 (5M)
Explain prototype evaluation methods.
Answer: Prototype evaluation checks whether a prototype solves the intended problem. Four common methods:
  1. Observation: Watch users interact without helping.
  2. Interviews: Ask users what they liked, disliked, and found confusing.
  3. Surveys: Collect ratings and structured feedback from many users.
  4. Metrics: Measure time to complete a task, error rate, task success rate.

Key principle: Measure behaviour, not opinions. What users do is more reliable than what they say they'd do.

Q6 (5M)
Explain the role of CAD, simulation and AI in prototyping.
Answer:

CAD (Computer-Aided Design): Software to create precise 3D models. Example: Fusion 360. Used for mechanical design, architecture, and product design.

Simulation: Virtual testing of a design under real conditions — stress, temperature, airflow. Example: ANSYS. Tesla simulates crash tests before building physical cars, saving millions.

AI-assisted visualization: Tools like Midjourney and DALL·E generate images from text prompts. Used for rapid concept visualization, colour variations, and client presentations.

Benefit: Digital tools let teams test hundreds of variations in hours instead of weeks.

Q7 (5M)
Explain the iteration log and its importance.
Answer: An iteration log records every change made to a prototype. Fields include:
  • Date — when the change was made.
  • Version — v1, v2, v3.
  • Change — what was changed.
  • Reason — why (usually from user feedback).
  • Result — what happened after.

Importance:

  • Team memory — what was tried and what worked.
  • Evidence of progress — for reports and pitches.
  • Prevents repeating failed experiments.
  • Shows discipline — a hallmark of good design teams.

📕 10-Mark Questions

Q1 (10M)
Discuss the different types of prototypes with real-world examples and their role in Design Thinking.
Answer:

Prototype: A rough version of a solution built to learn, test, and improve — not to ship.

Types of prototypes:

  1. Paper prototype: Hand-drawn screens. Cheap and fast. Used in early testing of apps.
  2. Physical prototype: A 3D model — cardboard, clay, or 3D printed. Used for industrial and product design.
  3. Digital prototype: Clickable Figma or Adobe XD mock-up. Used for apps and websites.
  4. Storyboard: Comic-strip sequence of the user journey. Used to plan before building.
  5. Role-play prototype: Team acts out the experience. Used for service design.
  6. Service prototype: Simulation with people and props — used to test invisible service steps.

Low vs high fidelity:

Rough, cheap, quickClose to final product

Real-world examples:

  • Dropbox — video MVP.
  • Amazon — paper prototypes for 1-Click.
  • IDEO — service role-plays for hospitals.

Conclusion: Prototypes let teams learn cheaply. The type chosen depends on what question needs answering.

Q2 (10M)
Discuss user testing methods and feedback collection. How does feedback drive iteration?
Answer:

User testing: Give the prototype to real users and watch them use it. Do not help. Observe and record.

Think-aloud method: Users speak their thoughts while using the prototype. Example: "I'm looking for the search button… I can't find it."

Feedback collection methods:

  • Surveys — quantitative ratings.
  • Interviews — qualitative depth.
  • Observation notes — real behaviour.
  • Metrics — task time, error rate, success rate.

Steps:

  1. Prepare tasks.
  2. Ask users to perform tasks.
  3. Observe and record.
  4. Ask questions after.
  5. Note problems.
  6. Iterate the prototype.

Real-world example — Amazon 1-Click: Amazon observed users abandoning carts at the payment step. They redesigned it into one-click ordering. This reduced friction and became a patented feature.

Diagram to draw: Prototype → Test → Observe → Feedback → Iterate → Prototype v2 (loop repeats)

Conclusion: Feedback is the raw material of iteration. Without it, designers build what they like, not what users need.

Q3 (10M)
Discuss iterative design, design critique and documentation. Give an example.
Answer:

Iterative design: Repeating the cycle design → prototype → test → learn → redesign. Each cycle is better because it's informed by real user feedback.

Design critique: A structured peer review. The team answers three questions: What works? What does not work? What can be improved? It catches problems early.

Documentation: Every change is recorded in an iteration log — date, version, change, reason, result.

Why documentation matters:

  • Team memory — knows what has been tried.
  • Evidence of progress — for reports.
  • Prevents repeating failed experiments.
  • Shows discipline to reviewers and clients.

Example — Instagram pivot:

  1. v1 was "Burbn" — a check-in app with many features.
  2. User testing showed people only used the photo-sharing feature.
  3. Design critique: cut everything else.
  4. Iteration: launched as Instagram — a photo app.
  5. Result: one of the fastest-growing apps in history.
Diagram to draw: Design → Prototype → Test → Critique → Learn → Redesign ↑______________________________________| (each loop is documented)

Conclusion: Iteration without critique is slow. Critique without iteration is wasted. Documentation ties both together.

Q4 (10M)
Discuss CAD, simulation and AI tools in modern prototyping with examples.
Answer:

CAD (Computer-Aided Design): Software to build precise 3D models. Tools: Fusion 360, AutoCAD, SolidWorks. Used for mechanical parts, architecture, and product design.

Simulation: Testing designs virtually under real-world conditions — stress, temperature, airflow. Tools: ANSYS, COMSOL. Example: Tesla simulates crash tests and aerodynamics in software before building physical cars, saving millions of dollars.

AI-assisted visualization: Tools like Midjourney and DALL·E generate images from prompts. Used for concept exploration, colour variations, and client presentations.

Benefits:

  • Speed — test hundreds of variations in hours.
  • Cost — no material or tooling costs for early tests.
  • Precision — CAD models are accurate to the millimetre.
  • Communication — AI visuals help non-designers understand concepts.

Limitations:

  • Simulation is only as accurate as its inputs.
  • AI images may not be buildable.
  • Digital models cannot test feel or emotion.
Diagram to draw: Idea → CAD model → Simulation → AI visualization → Physical prototype (form) (testing) (communication) (final check)

Conclusion: Modern digital tools accelerate prototyping, but physical testing remains essential for real-world validation.

Q5 (10M)
Discuss the complete prototyping process from concept development to validation. Illustrate with an example.
Answer: The prototyping process moves from rough ideas to validated solutions in cycles.

Stages:

  1. Concept development: Refine the chosen idea into a clear solution. Write down what it does, for whom, and why.
  2. Prototype mindset: Accept that the first version will be wrong. The goal is learning, not perfection.
  3. Choose fidelity: Start low-fidelity (paper) and increase only as ideas survive testing.
  4. Build prototype: Paper, physical, digital, storyboard, or role-play — whichever answers the current question.
  5. Design experimentation: Set up a test with real users. Prepare tasks.
  6. User testing: Watch users, use think-aloud, note friction points.
  7. Feedback collection: Surveys, interviews, metrics.
  8. Iteration: Update the prototype based on findings.
  9. Design critique: Peers review — what works, what doesn't, what can improve.
  10. Validation: Once the prototype consistently succeeds, it's ready to build.

Example — campus canteen app:

  • Concept: pre-order from phone, skip the queue.
  • Prototype v1: paper sketch of 5 screens.
  • Test: 5 students try it. Feedback: "Back button missing."
  • Prototype v2: added back button, enlarged wait-time chip.
  • Test v2: 4/5 students complete the task in under 60 seconds.
  • Validate: high task success rate → proceed to digital build.
Diagram to draw: Concept → Prototype → Test → Feedback → Iterate → Validate → Ship (v1) (v2, v3)

Conclusion: Prototyping is not a step — it's a continuous mindset. Every version teaches something the previous one couldn't.
