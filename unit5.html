§ 5.3

Technology vs Need-Driven; Sustainable & Frugal

ConceptK210 min
  • Technology-driven: Starts from a new technology.
  • Need-driven: Starts from a user need.
  • Sustainable innovation: Considers environment, society, economy.
  • Frugal innovation: Low-cost, simple, useful.
🚗 Tata Nano · Frugal Innovation
Ratan Tata set out to build a ₹1-lakh car for Indian families who couldn't afford cars. Everything was stripped down and rebuilt — the result was a breakthrough in frugal engineering.
Frugal innovation isn't cheap — it's clever.
💡 Examples

Technology-driven: New AI chip. Need-driven: Cheap water filter for villages. Frugal: Tata Nano.

📗 2-Mark Questions

Q1 (2M)
Differentiate between invention and innovation.
Answer: Invention is a new idea or creation; value may not yet be proven. Innovation is an invention that is implemented and creates real value. Example: telephone (invention) → smartphone (innovation).
Q2 (2M)
What is disruptive innovation? Give an example.
Answer: Disruptive innovation creates a new market by changing the rules. Example: Uber didn't build better taxis — it removed meters, cash, and waiting, reinventing the category.
Q3 (2M)
What is frugal innovation?
Answer: Frugal innovation delivers low-cost, simple, useful solutions. Example: Tata Nano — a ₹1-lakh car for Indian families.
Q4 (2M)
What does DFV stand for and why is it important?
Answer: DFV = Desirable, Feasible, Viable. An innovation must be all three: people want it, we can build it, and it's financially sustainable.
Q5 (2M)
What is a Value Proposition Canvas?
Answer: A two-sided tool: Customer Profile (Jobs, Pains, Gains) on one side, Value Map (Products, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators) on the other. The better the fit, the stronger the value.
Q6 (2M)
List any four blocks of the Business Model Canvas.
Answer: (1) Customer Segments, (2) Value Propositions, (3) Revenue Streams, (4) Cost Structure. The full BMC has 9 blocks.
Q7 (2M)
What is IPR? Name any three types.
Answer: IPR = Intellectual Property Rights — legal protection for creations. Three types: patents (inventions), trademarks (brand names and logos), copyright (creative works like books and songs).
Q8 (2M)
What is responsible innovation?
Answer: Responsible innovation considers ethics and societal impact — not just profit. It asks who benefits, who might be harmed, and whether the innovation respects human values.
Q9 (2M)
What are the six beats of an innovation pitch?
Answer: (1) Problem, (2) Solution, (3) Market, (4) Business Model, (5) Team, (6) Ask. In a 3-minute pitch, each gets about 30 seconds.
Q10 (2M)
What is the strongest signal of market validation?
Answer: Willingness to pay or pre-order. Compliments from friends mean little; money speaks. Users paying for a product proves real demand.

📘 5-Mark Questions

Q1 (5M)
Explain the different types of innovation with examples.
Answer: Innovation takes several forms:
  1. Incremental: Small improvement to existing product. Example: better phone battery.
  2. Disruptive: Creates new market by changing rules. Example: Uber replacing traditional taxis.
  3. Radical: Breakthrough that reshapes an industry. Example: the Internet.
  4. Product: A new product entirely. Example: the iPhone.
  5. Process: A new method of production. Example: assembly line.
  6. Service: A new service model. Example: Netflix streaming.
  7. Social: Solves a social issue. Example: microfinance.
Understanding the type helps match innovation to market readiness.
Q2 (5M)
Explain the DFV framework and its role in innovation.
Answer: DFV = Desirable, Feasible, Viable. All three must overlap for an innovation to succeed.
  1. Desirable: Do users want it? Validated through interviews, MVP tests, and willingness to pay.
  2. Feasible: Can we build it? Checked by engineering, supply chain, and technical capability.
  3. Viable: Can we sustain it financially? Checked through revenue model, cost structure, and unit economics.

Why it matters: Ideas fail when one of the three is missing — a desirable but infeasible product, a feasible but unviable one, or a viable one nobody wants.

Example: Netflix — Desirable (users want streaming), Feasible (broadband existed), Viable (subscription model).

Q3 (5M)
Explain the Value Proposition Canvas with an example.
Answer: The VPC has two sides that must fit together:

Customer Profile:

  • Jobs — what the customer is trying to do.
  • Pains — frustrations and obstacles.
  • Gains — desired outcomes.

Value Map:

  • Products — what you offer.
  • Pain Relievers — how you remove pains.
  • Gain Creators — how you deliver gains.

Example — Budget meal app:

  • Jobs: Eat quickly during a short break.
  • Pains: Long wait, high prices.
  • Gains: Cheap and fast.
  • Products: App listing nearby cheap food.
  • Pain Relievers: Quick delivery.
  • Gain Creators: Discounts and offers.

The better the fit between the two sides, the stronger the value proposition.

Q4 (5M)
Explain the 9 blocks of the Business Model Canvas.
Answer: The BMC is a one-page tool with nine blocks:
  1. Customer Segments: Who you serve.
  2. Value Propositions: What value you deliver.
  3. Channels: How you reach customers.
  4. Customer Relationships: How you keep them.
  5. Revenue Streams: How you earn.
  6. Key Resources: What assets you need.
  7. Key Activities: What you must do well.
  8. Key Partnerships: Who helps you.
  9. Cost Structure: What you spend.

Example — Spotify: Two customer segments (free and premium), value prop (music anywhere), revenue (ads + subscriptions), key activity (licensing music).

Q5 (5M)
Explain Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) and its four main types.
Answer: IPR protects creations and gives legal ownership. Four main types:
  1. Patent: Protects inventions — new processes, machines, compositions. Example: new machine design.
  2. Copyright: Protects creative works — books, songs, code, artwork. Example: a novel.
  3. Trademark: Protects brand names, logos, slogans. Example: Nike swoosh.
  4. Design registration: Protects visual appearance of a product. Example: shape of a bottle.

Why IPR matters:

  • Protects the inventor's effort.
  • Encourages innovation by rewarding risk.
  • Gives legal tools to fight copycats.

Example: Apple vs Samsung patent war reshaped how tech companies protect designs.

Q6 (5M)
Explain responsible, inclusive and sustainable innovation.
Answer:

Responsible innovation: Considers ethics and societal impact — not just profit. Asks who benefits, who might be harmed, and whether human values are respected.

Inclusive innovation: Designs for all people, including the poor, elderly, and disabled. Example: low-cost Braille e-readers for visually impaired readers.

Sustainable innovation: Considers environmental impact. Example: Patagonia's Worn Wear program repairs old jackets instead of selling new ones.

Innovation ecosystem: The network of government, universities, startups, and investors that supports innovation — like India's Atal Innovation Mission and Startup India.

Entrepreneurship: Turning innovation into a venture that creates jobs and value.

Q7 (5M)
Explain the structure and delivery of a 3-minute innovation pitch.
Answer: A 3-minute pitch follows six beats, roughly 30 seconds each:
  1. Problem (0:00–0:30): State the user pain with one real example.
  2. Solution (0:30–1:00): What your product does, in one clear sentence.
  3. Market (1:00–1:30): Size of the opportunity (users × price × frequency).
  4. Business Model (1:30–2:00): How you make money.
  5. Team (2:00–2:30): Who you are and why you can execute.
  6. Ask (2:30–3:00): Exactly what you need — funding, pilot, mentorship.

Delivery tips: Speak clearly, make eye contact, use simple slides, practice out loud, finish on time.

Q1 (10M)
Discuss the meaning and types of innovation with real-world examples. How does innovation differ from invention?
Answer:

Meaning: Innovation is a new idea that is implemented and creates value. It differs from invention — invention is the idea; innovation is the idea that reaches users and delivers value.

Comparison:

PointInventionInnovation
MeaningNew ideaNew idea + implementation
ValueMay not have value yetCreates value
ExampleTelephoneSmartphone

Types of innovation:

  1. Incremental: Small improvement. Example: better battery life.
  2. Disruptive: New market by new rules. Example: Uber.
  3. Radical: Breakthrough. Example: Internet.
  4. Product: New product. Example: iPhone.
  5. Process: New method. Example: assembly line.
  6. Service: New service. Example: Netflix.

Q2 (10M)
Discuss the DFV framework, Value Proposition Canvas, and Business Model Canvas. How do they work together?
Answer:

1. DFV Framework: Desirable, Feasible, Viable — the three tests an innovation must pass.

  • Desirable — do users want it?
  • Feasible — can we build it?
  • Viable — can we sustain it financially?

2. Value Proposition Canvas: Two sides — Customer Profile (Jobs, Pains, Gains) and Value Map (Products, Pain Relievers, Gain Creators). The fit between the two defines the strength of the value proposition.

Example — Budget meal app: Customer Profile — jobs: eat quickly; pains: long wait, high price; gains: cheap and fast. Value Map — products: app with nearby food; pain relievers: quick delivery; gain creators: discounts.

3. Business Model Canvas: A 9-block tool describing how the business creates and captures value: Customer Segments, Value Propositions, Channels, Customer Relationships, Revenue Streams, Key Resources, Key Activities, Key Partnerships, Cost Structure.

How they work together:

  • VPC answers what value we deliver.
  • BMC answers how the business delivers and captures that value.
  • DFV checks that the whole thing is desirable, feasible, and viable.
Diagram to draw: VPC (Value) → BMC (Business) → DFV (Sanity Check) → Innovation

Conclusion: The three tools together convert an idea into a validated, sustainable innovation.

Q3 (10M)
Discuss Intellectual Property Rights (IPR). Explain patents, copyright, trademarks, and design protection with examples.
Answer:

IPR definition: Intellectual Property Rights are legal protections for creations of the mind — inventions, art, brands, and designs. They give owners exclusive rights for a limited time.

Four types:

  1. Patent: Protects inventions — new processes, machines, compositions of matter. Duration: usually 20 years. Example: new drug formula.
  2. Copyright: Protects creative works — books, songs, code, films, artwork. Duration: typically life of author + 60 years. Example: a novel or an app's source code.
  3. Trademark: Protects brand identifiers — names, logos, slogans. Duration: renewable indefinitely. Example: Nike's swoosh, Apple's bitten apple.
  4. Design registration: Protects the visual appearance — shape, pattern, ornamentation. Duration: 10–15 years. Example: the shape of a Coca-Cola bottle.

Why IPR matters:

  • Protects the creator's effort and investment.
  • Encourages innovation by rewarding risk.
  • Gives legal tools against copycats.
  • Can be licensed or sold — a business asset.

Example — Apple vs Samsung: Apple sued Samsung over design patents and trade dress. The multi-billion dollar case reshaped how tech companies protect and license their IPR globally.

Diagram to draw: Invention → Patent (⚙️) Creative → Copyright (📖) Brand → Trademark (™️) Appearance → Design (🎨)

Conclusion: IPR turns innovation into a defensible asset — crucial for startups and creators.

Q4 (10M)
Discuss responsible, inclusive, and sustainable innovation with examples. What is an innovation ecosystem?
Answer:

1. Responsible innovation: Considers ethics and societal impact — not just profit. It asks: Who benefits? Who might be harmed? Are human values respected? Example: AI with bias audits before deployment.

2. Inclusive innovation: Designs for everyone, including the poor, elderly, and disabled. Example: low-cost Braille e-readers for visually impaired readers.

3. Sustainable innovation: Considers environmental impact. Example: Patagonia's Worn Wear program repairs old jackets instead of selling new ones — lower profit, lower environmental impact, stronger customer loyalty.

4. Innovation ecosystem: The network of government, universities, startups, investors, and mentors that supports innovation. In India:

  • Atal Innovation Mission (NITI Aayog): Funds tinkering labs and startups.
  • Startup India: Tax benefits and simplified registration for startups.
  • National Innovation Foundation: Supports grassroots inventors.

5. Entrepreneurship: Turning innovation into a venture. Creates jobs, solves problems at scale, and attracts investment.

Example: A startup building low-cost water filters for rural India works with IIT incubators (ecosystem) and follows frugal, sustainable, inclusive principles.

Diagram to draw: Government ─ Universities ─ Investors ↓ Startup / Entrepreneur ↓ Responsible + Inclusive + Sustainable Innovation ↓ Societal Value

Conclusion: Innovation at its best is not just clever — it's responsible, inclusive, and sustainable.

Q5 (10M)
Discuss the structure, delivery and importance of a 3-minute innovation pitch. Give a complete example.
Answer:

Importance: A pitch converts weeks of design work into a 3-minute story that wins funding, partnerships, or approval. It is the final step that turns a prototype into an innovation.

Structure — Six Beats (30 seconds each):

  1. Problem (0:00–0:30): State the user pain with one real example.
  2. Solution (0:30–1:00): What your product does, in one clear sentence.
  3. Market (1:00–1:30): Size of the opportunity (users × price × frequency).
  4. Business Model (1:30–2:00): How you make money.
  5. Team (2:00–2:30): Who you are and why you can execute.
  6. Ask (2:30–3:00): Exactly what you need — funding, pilot, mentorship.

Delivery tips: Speak clearly, make eye contact, use simple slides, practice out loud, finish on time.

Full example — SkipQ (campus canteen app):

  • Problem: 800 students lose 10+ minutes daily to canteen queues.
  • Solution: SkipQ — scan a QR, order, pick up. No queue.
  • Market: 800 students × ₹40 × 200 days = ₹64 lakh/year.
  • Model: ₹2/order convenience fee; canteen keeps 50%.
  • Team: 3 engineering students + 1 canteen partner.
  • Ask: ₹15,000 for domain + server for 6 months. Pilot with 1 canteen in 2 weeks.

Feedback loop: Listen carefully, note questions, improve the pitch and the product.

Diagram to draw: 0:00 ─ Problem ─ 0:30 ─ Solution ─ 1:00 ─ Market ─ 1:30 ─ Model ─ 2:00 ─ Team ─ 2:30 ─ Ask ─ 3:00

Conclusion: The best pitch isn't the flashiest — it's the clearest. If your grandmother doesn't understand it, investors won't either.
